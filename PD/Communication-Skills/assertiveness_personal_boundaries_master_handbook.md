# 1. What Assertiveness Means

**Assertiveness** is the ability to express your needs, opinions, preferences, limits, concerns, and rights clearly while still respecting the rights and dignity of other people.

Assertiveness is not about winning every conversation.

It is not about forcing people to agree with you.

It is not about becoming rude, dominant, cold, or selfish.

It means being able to say:

- what you think,
- what you need,
- what you are willing to do,
- what you are not willing to do,
- what behavior you accept,
- what behavior you do not accept,
- and what action you will take if a situation continues.

A useful summary is:

> **Assertiveness = honesty + clarity + respect + appropriate firmness.**

### Example

Passive response:

> “Okay, I’ll do it.”

even though you already have too much work.

Aggressive response:

> “Stop dumping your work on me. Do it yourself.”

Assertive response:

> “I’m already working on two high-priority tasks. I can take this on after 3 PM, or we can decide which current task should move.”

The assertive response communicates the problem without attacking the other person.

# 2. Why Assertiveness and Boundaries Matter

Healthy assertiveness helps protect:

- your time,
- your energy,
- your workload,
- your mental space,
- your responsibilities,
- your privacy,
- your values,
- your relationships,
- your professional reputation,
- and your ability to make deliberate choices.

Without boundaries, people may gradually learn that your time or availability is unlimited.

This does not always happen because people are intentionally disrespectful. Sometimes unclear expectations create the problem.

### Example

If you answer work messages at midnight every day, coworkers may eventually assume:

> “Shoeb normally responds late at night.”

The pattern itself becomes an expectation.

A boundary might instead be:

> “I usually disconnect after 7 PM. If something is genuinely urgent, please call.”

Now the expectation is clearer.

# 3. Passive vs Aggressive vs Passive-Aggressive vs Assertive Behavior

Understanding these four communication styles is fundamental.

## 3.1 Passive behavior

Passive behavior occurs when a person regularly suppresses their needs or preferences to avoid disagreement, rejection, criticism, or discomfort.

Common passive patterns:

- saying yes when you want to say no,
- apologizing excessively,
- avoiding necessary conversations,
- remaining silent when treated unfairly,
- letting others decide everything,
- accepting unreasonable requests,
- giving indirect hints instead of speaking clearly,
- building resentment without communicating.

### Passive example

Manager:

> “Can you finish this report tonight?”

Employee:

> “Sure.”

Internally:

> “I already promised my family I would leave early. Why does this always happen?”

The employee avoids immediate discomfort but may create resentment.

---

## 3.2 Aggressive behavior

Aggressive communication prioritizes your needs while ignoring or disrespecting another person’s rights.

Common aggressive behaviors:

- shouting,
- insulting,
- threatening,
- humiliating,
- interrupting,
- demanding,
- blaming,
- using fear,
- attacking someone’s character,
- making unnecessarily harsh statements.

### Aggressive example

> “Your planning is terrible. I’m not fixing your problem.”

The underlying boundary may be reasonable, but the delivery is unnecessarily hostile.

---

## 3.3 Passive-aggressive behavior

Passive-aggressive behavior expresses resistance indirectly instead of communicating clearly.

Examples:

- deliberately delaying work,
- sarcasm,
- silent treatment,
- saying “fine” while acting resentful,
- “forgetting” tasks intentionally,
- indirect criticism,
- making jokes that contain hidden anger.

### Example

Instead of saying:

> “I’m frustrated that you changed the deadline without discussing it.”

a passive-aggressive person may say:

> “Amazing planning as usual.”

The frustration is real, but the message is indirect.

---

## 3.4 Assertive behavior

Assertive communication is direct but respectful.

Example:

> “The deadline changed from Friday to Wednesday. I can complete the testing by Wednesday, but the documentation would need to move to Thursday. Which should take priority?”

This message:

1. describes the situation,
2. explains the limitation,
3. proposes realistic options,
4. asks for a decision.

---

## Quick Comparison

| Style | Main Focus | Typical Message | Common Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Passive | Avoid conflict | “Whatever you want.” | Resentment, overload |
| Aggressive | Control others | “Do it my way.” | Fear, conflict |
| Passive-aggressive | Indirect resistance | “Sure, whatever.” | Confusion, distrust |
| Assertive | Respect both sides | “Here is what I can do.” | Clarity, negotiation |

# 4. Rights, Responsibilities, and Mutual Respect

Assertiveness works best when you remember that both people have rights.

You have the right to:

- express an opinion,
- ask questions,
- request clarification,
- say no,
- change your mind,
- ask for reasonable time,
- protect private information,
- make mistakes,
- choose your priorities,
- ask for respectful communication,
- disagree respectfully.

Other people have the same rights.

This means:

> You may say no to someone, and they may feel disappointed.

Their disappointment does not automatically mean your boundary is wrong.

Likewise:

> You may make a request, and they may say no.

Assertiveness includes accepting another person's reasonable boundary.

# 5. Understanding Personal Boundaries

A **boundary** is a limit that defines what you will accept, participate in, provide, share, tolerate, or allow in a particular situation.

A boundary is usually about **your own behavior or participation**, not about controlling someone else.

Weak control statement:

> “You are not allowed to talk to your friends.”

Boundary:

> “I am not comfortable remaining in a relationship where I am repeatedly insulted in front of other people.”

The second statement explains what **you will or will not participate in**.


## 5.1 Boundary vs Request vs Rule

These ideas are easy to confuse.

| Concept | Main purpose | Who controls the action? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Request | Ask another person to do something | The other person decides | "Could you lower your voice while I am on this call?" |
| Rule | Define behavior within a legitimate system or role | The person or organization with authority enforces it | "Visitors must sign in at reception." |
| Boundary | Define your own participation, access, time, property, or response | You control your response | "If shouting starts, I will end the conversation and continue later." |
| Threat | Use fear or punishment mainly to force compliance | Attempts to control the other person | "Do what I want or I will make you regret it." |

A boundary can include a request, but it should not depend entirely on the other person's cooperation.

## 5.2 How a Boundary Works

A practical boundary process is:

1. **Notice the problem.** Identify the behavior or situation that is creating difficulty.
2. **Identify your limit.** Decide what you can reasonably accept and what you cannot.
3. **Communicate clearly.** State the limit without unnecessary accusation.
4. **Choose a response you control.** Decide what you will do if the situation continues.
5. **Follow through consistently.** A boundary that is repeatedly announced but never acted on may become unclear.
6. **Review the result.** Adjust the boundary if it is unrealistic, too vague, or no longer needed.

### Workplace example

> "I can review urgent production issues after hours, but routine requests sent at night will be handled the next working day."

The boundary does not forbid coworkers from sending messages. It defines when you will normally respond.

## 5.3 What Boundaries Cannot Guarantee

A boundary cannot guarantee that:

- another person will agree;
- nobody will feel disappointed;
- conflict will disappear;
- people will immediately change their habits.

The purpose is to make **your limits and choices clearer**, not to guarantee a particular reaction.

## Common mistakes

- setting a boundary only after resentment has built for months;
- using vague language such as "respect my time" without explaining what that means;
- creating consequences you cannot or will not carry out;
- confusing preference with a non-negotiable limit;
- changing the boundary every time someone pushes back;
- using "boundary" as a label for controlling another adult's ordinary choices.

## Best practice

Start with the **least restrictive boundary that adequately protects the legitimate need**. Increase firmness when repeated behavior, risk, or disrespect makes that necessary.

# 6. Types of Boundaries

## 6.1 Time boundaries

Protect how your time is used.

Examples:

- when you are available,
- how long you can stay,
- how much notice you need,
- when work ends.

Script:

> “I can help for 20 minutes, but after that I need to return to my own task.”

---

## 6.2 Workload boundaries

Define how much work you can reasonably accept.

Script:

> “I can take this task, but I would need to move the API testing to tomorrow.”

---

## 6.3 Emotional boundaries

Protect you from being made responsible for another adult's emotions.

Example:

You can listen to a friend, but you do not have to be available every hour.

Script:

> “I care about what you’re going through, but I don’t have the capacity for a long conversation tonight. Can we talk tomorrow?”

---

## 6.4 Physical boundaries

Relate to personal space, touch, possessions, and physical privacy.

Script:

> “Please ask before using my desk.”

---

## 6.5 Privacy boundaries

Define what personal information you share.

Script:

> “I prefer to keep that private.”

No further explanation is required.

---

## 6.6 Digital boundaries

Examples:

- notification expectations,
- password sharing,
- response times,
- group chats,
- social media tagging,
- work messages after hours.

Script:

> “I don’t keep work notifications enabled after 8 PM. I’ll respond the next morning.”

---

## 6.7 Financial boundaries

Examples:

- lending money,
- paying shared expenses,
- buying gifts,
- financial disclosure.

Script:

> “I’m not able to lend money, but I can help you think through other options.”

---

## 6.8 Values boundaries

These protect your principles.

Example:

> “I’m not comfortable falsifying the report.”

---

## 6.9 Conversation boundaries

Examples:

- shouting,
- insults,
- personal attacks,
- repeated interrogation,
- gossip.

Script:

> “I’m willing to discuss the issue, but I won’t continue the conversation while we’re insulting each other.”

# 7. Healthy, Rigid, and Porous Boundaries

Boundaries can be too weak or too inflexible.

## Porous boundaries

Too open.

Examples:

- saying yes to everything,
- oversharing,
- tolerating disrespect,
- constantly changing limits under pressure.

## Rigid boundaries

Too closed.

Examples:

- refusing reasonable help,
- never compromising,
- avoiding emotional closeness,
- treating every request as an intrusion.

## Healthy boundaries

Flexible but clear.

Example:

> “Normally I don’t work on weekends, but I can support this Saturday because we have a production release. I’ll take Monday morning off in return.”

This shows flexibility without creating unlimited availability.

# 8. How to Identify Your Boundaries

Many people struggle to set boundaries because they have never clearly defined them.

Look for signals such as:

- repeated irritation,
- dread,
- resentment,
- feeling taken advantage of,
- exhaustion,
- repeatedly saying “I wish they would stop…”,
- wishing you had said no,
- feeling pressured to explain everything,
- frequently sacrificing important priorities.

These signals do not automatically prove someone has crossed a boundary, but they tell you to investigate.

## Boundary discovery questions

Ask yourself:

1. What exactly happened?
2. What part bothered me?
3. Was my expectation communicated?
4. What do I want to happen differently?
5. What am I willing to do?
6. What am I not willing to do?
7. Is my request reasonable?
8. What will I do if the behavior continues?

### Example

Problem:

> Coworkers keep scheduling meetings during your lunch.

Possible boundary:

> “I block 1:00–1:30 PM for lunch unless there is an urgent production issue.”

# 9. The Anatomy of an Assertive Message

A useful assertive message often contains four parts:

## 1. Observation

Describe what happened without exaggeration.

> “The requirements were changed yesterday.”

Avoid:

> “You always change everything at the last second.”

## 2. Impact

Explain the practical effect.

> “That means the current estimate is no longer realistic.”

## 3. Boundary or need

State what you need.

> “I need one additional day to complete testing properly.”

## 4. Next step

Offer a decision or action.

> “We can deliver Thursday, or reduce the scope for Wednesday.”

### Full example

> “The requirements changed yesterday, so the current estimate is no longer realistic. I need one additional day to complete testing properly. We can deliver the full scope Thursday, or reduce the scope for Wednesday.”

This structure is especially useful at work.

# 10. Using “I” Statements Correctly

“I” statements focus on your perspective.

Useful form:

> “I feel/need/prefer ___ when ___ because ___.”

Example:

> “I find it difficult to concentrate when conversations happen beside my desk. Could we use the meeting room for longer discussions?”

Bad “I” statement:

> “I feel that you are selfish.”

This is still a judgment.

Better:

> “I felt excluded when the decision was made without me even though I’m responsible for implementation.”


## 10.1 A More Precise Four-Part Structure

A useful "I" statement can be built from four parts:

1. **Observation** — what happened, stated as neutrally as possible;
2. **Impact or feeling** — how it affected you or the work;
3. **Need or concern** — what matters in the situation;
4. **Request** — what you would like to happen next.

Template:

> "When [specific behavior/situation] happens, I [feel/experience impact] because [need or reason]. Could we [specific request]?"

Example:

> "When requirements change after estimates are finalized, I have difficulty giving a reliable delivery date because the scope has changed. Could we reconfirm the priority and timeline before I recommit?"

## 10.2 Feelings vs Judgments

Some sentences begin with "I feel" but still contain accusations.

Judgment disguised as feeling:

> "I feel that you don't care about the team."

Actual feeling or impact:

> "I felt frustrated when the decision was made without the people who have to implement it."

The second version is easier to discuss because it points to a specific event rather than claiming to know another person's motives.

## When "I" statements are useful

Use them when:

- describing personal impact;
- raising a sensitive concern;
- explaining a preference or limit;
- reducing unnecessary blame;
- asking for a change in behavior.

## When not to force the formula

You do not need an "I" statement for every ordinary fact or operational instruction.

Instead of:

> "I feel that the server is unavailable."

say:

> "The server is unavailable."

Assertiveness should sound natural, not scripted.

## Common mistakes

- making the statement too long;
- adding "always" or "never" accusations;
- guessing motives;
- describing a vague problem with no request;
- using a calm formula while delivering it sarcastically.

A good "I" statement improves clarity; it does not automatically make the other person agree.

# 11. Tone, Body Language, and Delivery

Words are only part of assertiveness.

Your delivery matters.

## Helpful behaviors

- calm voice,
- normal speaking volume,
- steady pace,
- direct but natural eye contact,
- relaxed posture,
- neutral facial expression,
- short sentences,
- minimal unnecessary apology.

## Avoid

- whispering,
- laughing nervously while setting a serious boundary,
- shouting,
- pointing fingers,
- standing unnecessarily close,
- sarcastic tone,
- giving a 10-minute explanation for a simple no.

### Example

Less effective:

> “Umm, sorry, actually maybe if it’s okay, I kind of can’t do that, sorry.”

Clearer:

> “I can’t take that on today.”

# 12. How to Say No

Saying no is one of the most important boundary skills.

A no can be:

- polite,
- brief,
- specific,
- final.

A common formula:

> **Acknowledge + No + Optional reason + Optional alternative**

Example:

> “Thanks for asking. I can’t join tonight because I already have plans. I’d be happy to meet next weekend.”

You do not need to provide an alternative every time.

### Simple no

> “No, I’m not able to do that.”

### Professional no

> “I don’t have capacity to take this on today.”

### Privacy no

> “I’d rather not discuss that.”

### Financial no

> “I’m not able to lend money.”

---

# 13. Different Ways to Say No

## Direct no

> “No, I can’t.”

Use when the situation is simple.

## No with appreciation

> “Thanks for thinking of me, but I’ll pass.”

## No with reason

> “I can’t stay late today because I have another commitment.”

## No with alternative

> “I can’t review it today, but I can look at it tomorrow morning.”

## Conditional yes

> “I can do that if the deadline moves to Friday.”

## Limited yes

> “I can help for 30 minutes.”

## Priority trade-off

> “I can take this, but one of my current tasks will need to move. Which should I deprioritize?”

This is especially valuable in the workplace.

# 14. Setting Expectations Clearly

Many boundary problems begin as expectation problems.

Do not assume people know:

- when you are available,
- how quickly you reply,
- what you are responsible for,
- what you consider urgent,
- how much notice you need.

State expectations early.

### Example: project communication

> “I’ll review pull requests twice a day—once around noon and once before 5 PM. For production blockers, message me directly.”

### Example: shared apartment

> “If guests are staying overnight, can we give each other a day’s notice?”

### Example: friendship

> “I may not reply immediately during work hours, but I’ll get back to you later.”

# 15. Protecting Your Time

Time boundaries protect your priorities.

Common time problems:

- unplanned meetings,
- endless calls,
- “quick favors”,
- interruptions,
- tasks with unclear ownership,
- after-hours communication,
- people arriving late,
- overcommitting.

## Useful techniques

### Time-box help

> “I have 15 minutes now. Show me where you’re stuck.”

### Set an ending time

> “I can stay until 6:30.”

### Require notice

> “For requests like this, please give me at least one day’s notice when possible.”

### Protect focus blocks

> “I’m heads-down until 2 PM. I’ll check messages after that.”

### Decline unnecessary meetings

> “I don’t think I’m needed for the full meeting. Please send me the action item, or invite me only for the technical section.”

# 16. Handling Pressure, Persistence, and Pushback

Some people accept a boundary immediately.

Others push back.

Typical reactions:

- “Come on.”
- “It will only take five minutes.”
- “You always used to help.”
- “Why are you being difficult?”
- “Everyone else does it.”
- “Just this once.”

The mistake many people make is assuming that every objection requires a new explanation.

Often it does not.

### Example

Person:

> “Can you work this weekend?”

You:

> “I’m not available this weekend.”

Person:

> “But we really need you.”

You:

> “I understand. I’m still not available this weekend.”

Person:

> “Can you at least log in Sunday morning?”

You:

> “No. I can pick it up Monday morning.”

This is assertive repetition.

# 17. The Broken-Record Technique

The **broken-record technique** means calmly repeating the same core message without being pulled into endless arguments.

It is useful when:

- someone keeps pressuring you,
- someone tries to distract from the main point,
- you have already explained your position,
- further debate is not productive.

### Example

Salesperson:

> “You’ll lose the offer if you don’t sign today.”

You:

> “I don’t make this kind of decision immediately. I’ll review it first.”

Salesperson:

> “There’s really nothing to review.”

You:

> “I understand. I’ll still review it first.”

Salesperson:

> “Can you at least pay the deposit?”

You:

> “No. I’ll review it first.”

Do not add anger unless necessary.

# 18. Workplace Boundaries

Workplace assertiveness is not about refusing work.

It is about making capacity, ownership, deadlines, priorities, and expectations visible.

Healthy workplace boundaries include:

- reasonable working hours,
- realistic workload,
- clear responsibilities,
- respectful communication,
- meeting boundaries,
- escalation procedures,
- privacy,
- appropriate use of personal devices,
- breaks and leave.

### Weak workplace boundary

> “I’ll somehow manage.”

### Stronger workplace boundary

> “I can complete A and B today. If C is also required today, I need guidance on which current task should move.”

The second statement helps the organization make a priority decision.

# 19. Boundaries With Managers

Setting boundaries with managers often feels difficult because of the power difference.

The key is to frame the conversation around:

- priorities,
- delivery quality,
- capacity,
- risk,
- trade-offs,
- business impact.

## Scenario: too many urgent tasks

Manager:

> “I need these three items today.”

Response:

> “I can finish the deployment script and production issue today. The dashboard change will need to move to tomorrow unless we reduce the scope. Which option would you prefer?”

## Scenario: frequent after-hours messages

> “I’ve noticed several requests are coming after 9 PM. For normal work I’ll pick them up the next morning. If there’s a production emergency, please call me so I know it requires immediate attention.”

## Scenario: unrealistic deadline

> “With the current scope, Friday is not realistic without skipping testing. My recommendation is Monday. If Friday is fixed, we need to reduce the scope.”

This is stronger than simply saying:

> “I can’t.”

because it explains the trade-off.

# 20. Boundaries With Coworkers

Common coworker boundary issues include:

- repeatedly asking for help,
- assigning work without authority,
- interrupting,
- taking credit,
- borrowing items,
- gossip,
- sharing private information,

## Scenario: coworker assigning work

> “I’m currently committed to tasks from my manager. Please coordinate with them if this needs to become a priority.”

## Scenario: coworker takes credit

> “I want to clarify that I implemented the API integration and Rahul handled testing. Let’s make sure the project summary reflects both contributions accurately.”

# 21. Meetings, Messages, Email, and Availability Boundaries

## Meetings

Ask:

- Is my presence necessary?
- Is there an agenda?
- Can the decision be made asynchronously?
- Do I need the entire meeting?

Script:

> “I can join for the first 20 minutes to cover the architecture decision, then I need to leave for another priority.”

## Chat

Set response expectations.

> “I may not respond immediately while coding. If something is blocking production, tag me as urgent.”

## Email

You do not have to answer every email immediately.

Use folders, priority rules, scheduled checks, or response windows.

## Personal messaging

> “I usually keep my phone away while working, so my replies may be delayed.”

# 22. Workload, Deadlines, and Priority Negotiation

One of the best assertiveness skills at work is **trade-off communication**.

Instead of:

> “No, I’m too busy.”

Say:

> “I can do X, but Y will move. Which is more important?”

### Priority negotiation formula

> **Current commitments + new request + trade-off + decision needed**

Example:

> “I’m currently finishing the release fix and invoice testing. I can start the report today, but invoice testing would move to tomorrow. Which should take priority?”

### Why this works

It turns a personal conflict into a planning decision.

Examples include boundaries around:

- privacy,
- communication,
- time,
- friendships,
- personal space,
- money,
- social media,
- conflict,
- family involvement,
- personal goals.

## Example: privacy

> “I’m happy to share what I’m comfortable with, but I don’t want us checking each other’s phones.”

## Example: conflict

> “I want to resolve this, but I’m not willing to continue while we’re shouting. I’ll come back to the conversation when we can speak calmly.”

## Example: time

> “I value spending time together, and I also need some time each week for my own activities.”

# 24. Family and Friendship Boundaries

Family boundaries can be difficult because old patterns may have existed for years.

## Unwanted advice

> “I know you’re trying to help. I’ve decided how I want to handle this, so I’m not looking for advice on it right now.”

## Personal questions

> “I’d rather not discuss my salary.”

## Surprise visits

> “Please call before coming over. I may not be available otherwise.”

## Friend repeatedly cancelling

> “I’m happy to meet, but repeated last-minute cancellations make planning difficult. Let’s confirm on the day before I block the time.”

## Friend expects constant availability

> “I care about you, but I can’t always talk immediately. If I don’t reply, it usually means I’m busy and I’ll respond later.”

---

# 25. Privacy and Digital Boundaries

Digital access can create an illusion of permanent availability.

Healthy digital boundaries may include:

- disabling notifications,
- not sharing passwords,
- limiting location sharing,
- restricting social tagging,
- avoiding work chats during leave,
- using “Do Not Disturb,”
- separating work and personal accounts.

### Example: password request

> “I don’t share my passwords. If you need access, I can log in myself or we can create a separate account.”

### Example: social media posting

> “Please ask before posting photos of me.”

### Example: work during leave

> “I’ll be offline during my leave. Please contact the support rotation for operational issues.”

# 26. Money, Favors, and Resource Boundaries

Money can create emotional pressure.

A clear financial boundary may be necessary even with close relationships.

## Lending money

> “I’m not in a position to lend money.”

You do not need to reveal your bank balance to justify it.

## Repeated borrowing

> “I’m not comfortable lending money again while the previous amount is still unpaid.”

## Shared bills

> “Let’s split the rent and utilities according to the arrangement we agreed.”

## Expensive gift pressure

> “That’s outside my budget, so I won’t be joining that purchase.”

# 27. Handling Guilt

Many people know what boundary they want but struggle because of guilt.

Common thoughts:

- “They will think I’m selfish.”
- “Maybe I should just do it.”
- “They helped me before.”
- “Good people always help.”
- “What if they get angry?”

Important distinction:

> **Guilt is a feeling, not automatically evidence that you did something wrong.**

You can feel guilty while making a reasonable decision.

### Evaluate guilt with questions

1. Did I behave disrespectfully?
2. Did I break an agreement?
3. Did I intentionally harm someone?
4. Or did I simply disappoint someone?

Disappointing someone is not automatically wrongdoing.

### Example

You decline an invitation because you need rest.

You may feel guilty.

But you did not necessarily do anything wrong.

---

# 28. Dealing With Disappointment and Conflict

A boundary can create temporary conflict even when it is reasonable.

Someone may be:

- surprised,
- disappointed,
- annoyed,
- confused.

You do not have to remove every negative emotion another person experiences.

But you can acknowledge it.

### Example

> “I understand this is frustrating. I’m still not able to take it on tonight.”

This combines empathy with firmness.

Avoid:

> “Fine, I’ll do it.”

if you do not mean it.

---

# 29. Handling Manipulation and Boundary Testing

Not every disagreement is manipulation.

However, be cautious when people repeatedly use tactics such as:

- guilt,
- threats,
- intimidation,
- humiliation,
- silent treatment,
- repeated pressure,
- “If you cared about me, you would…”
- denying previous agreements,
- involving third parties to pressure you,
- making you responsible for their choices.

## Example

> “If you were really my friend, you’d lend me the money.”

Assertive response:

> “Our friendship isn’t determined by whether I lend money. I’m not able to lend it.”

## Example

> “Everyone else stays late.”

Response:

> “I understand. I’m not available after 7 tonight.”

---

# 30. When You Need to Escalate

Communication does not solve every situation.

At work, escalation may be appropriate when there is:

- repeated harassment,
- discrimination,
- threats,
- retaliation,
- serious safety concerns,
- repeated policy violations,
- ongoing behavior after clear requests,
- unethical or illegal instructions.

Possible steps may include:

1. document dates and facts,
2. keep relevant messages,
3. communicate the issue clearly if safe,
4. consult your manager,
5. use HR or the appropriate reporting channel,
6. follow organizational policy.

For personal relationships, if a situation includes threats, stalking, coercion, violence, or serious safety concerns, prioritize safety rather than trying to “communicate better.”

# 31. Apologies, Mistakes, and Resetting Boundaries

Assertiveness does not mean you are always correct.

If you made a mistake:

> “I agreed to the deadline too quickly. After reviewing the work, I realize Friday isn’t realistic. I should have checked before committing. The earliest reliable delivery is Monday.”

This combines accountability with a new boundary.

## Changing a previous pattern

You can also change a boundary.

> “I know I used to answer work messages late at night, but I’m changing that routine. Going forward I’ll respond during working hours unless there’s an emergency.”

Previous availability does not create a permanent obligation.


## 31.1 A Simple Repair-and-Reset Process

When your own mistake contributed to the problem, use:

**Acknowledge → Own → Correct → Restate → Follow through**

### Example

> "I said yes before checking my calendar, and that created confusion. That was my mistake. I cannot attend Thursday evening, but I can join Friday morning. In future I will check before confirming."

This does two things at once: it repairs the mistake and sets a clearer expectation.

## 31.2 You Can Apologize Without Abandoning the Boundary

An apology is appropriate for **how you handled something** when that handling was poor. It does not mean the underlying limit must disappear.

Example:

> "I should not have snapped at you, and I'm sorry for that. I still need us to agree before borrowing each other's belongings."

The apology addresses the disrespectful delivery. The boundary remains.

## 31.3 Resetting an Inconsistent Boundary

Sometimes you created the expectation yourself by repeatedly making exceptions.

Use three steps:

1. acknowledge the old pattern;
2. state what is changing;
3. explain the new default.

> "I've often accepted last-minute review requests, so I understand why that became normal. I need to change that. Going forward, please send routine reviews at least one working day before the deadline."

## If the other person says, "But you allowed it before"

You can answer:

> "That's true. I allowed it before, and I'm changing what I can commit to now."

Past permission does not necessarily create permanent permission.

## Best practices

- correct the mistake without excessive self-criticism;
- state the new boundary once, clearly;
- avoid inventing an emergency consequence just to sound firm;
- expect a transition period when a long-standing pattern changes;
- demonstrate the new boundary through consistent behavior.

# 32. Common Assertiveness Mistakes

## Mistake 1: Overexplaining

Weak:

> “I’m really sorry, because my cousin might visit and I have to maybe help my parents…”

Better:

> “I’m not available this weekend.”

---

## Mistake 2: Asking permission for your boundary

Weak:

> “Would it maybe be okay if I didn’t answer after 10?”

Better:

> “I won’t be available after 10 PM.”

---

## Mistake 3: Turning boundaries into threats

Threat:

> “If you ever do that again, you’ll regret it.”

Boundary:

> “If the insults continue, I’ll end the conversation.”

---

## Mistake 4: Expecting instant agreement

A boundary can still be valid even when someone dislikes it.

---

## Mistake 5: Being vague

Vague:

> “Please respect my time.”

Specific:

> “Please check with me before booking meetings after 6 PM.”

---

## Mistake 6: Setting consequences you will not follow

If you say:

> “If shouting continues, I will leave the room.”

then repeatedly stay for hours, the boundary becomes less credible.

Choose consequences you can realistically enforce.

---

## Mistake 7: Confusing assertiveness with harshness

You do not need to be rude to be firm.

## The five-second assertiveness check

Before responding, ask:

1. What do I actually want?
2. Do I have capacity?
3. What is my responsibility?
4. What trade-off would this create?

## The ASSERT Framework

Use this simple framework for difficult situations:

### A — Assess
What exactly is happening?

### S — State facts
Describe observable behavior.

### S — Specify your need
What do you want to change?

### E — Explain impact
Why does it matter?

### R — Request or boundary
What are you asking for?

### T — Take action
What will you do if needed?

### Example

> “The meeting has moved past the scheduled end time. I have another commitment at 4 PM, so I need to leave now. Please send me any action items afterward.”

# 37. Final Principles

Remember these principles:

1. **Clear is usually kinder than vague.**
2. **Saying no does not automatically make you selfish.**
3. **You are responsible for communicating your limits.**
4. **Other people are allowed to dislike your boundary.**
5. **A boundary is not the same as controlling another person.**
6. **Do not set consequences you cannot realistically enforce.**
7. **Use calm repetition when pressure continues.**
8. **At work, discuss trade-offs instead of silently accepting impossible workloads.**
9. **Healthy relationships can survive reasonable boundaries.**
10. **You can change a boundary when circumstances change.**
11. **You can apologize for your delivery without abandoning the boundary itself.**
12. **Assertiveness is a skill and improves with practice.**
13. **Respect other people's boundaries just as you expect them to respect yours.**
14. **Small boundaries prevent larger resentment.**
15. **Safety, ethics, and serious misconduct may require escalation rather than continued negotiation.**

# Appendix C — Boundary Decision Tree

When someone asks you for something:

**Step 1: Is it your responsibility?**

- If yes, continue.
- If no, decide whether you voluntarily want to help.

**Step 2: Do you have capacity?**

- If yes, consider accepting.
- If no, negotiate scope, deadline, or priority.

**Step 3: Does it violate a personal value, policy, or safety requirement?**

- If yes, decline or escalate.

**Step 4: Are you saying yes because you genuinely choose to, or because you fear disapproval?**

- If fear is the main reason, pause before committing.

**Step 5: What is the shortest clear response?**

Use it.

# Closing Thought

A healthy boundary is not a wall against other people.

It is a clear line that helps you decide:

> **What is mine to handle? What is not mine? What am I willing to offer? What do I need to protect?**

The goal of assertiveness is not to become harder.

The goal is to become **clearer, more responsible, more respectful, and more deliberate** in how you deal with yourself and others.
