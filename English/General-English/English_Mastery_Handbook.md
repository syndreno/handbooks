# 1. How to Use This Handbook

English has several different skills:

- Grammar
- Vocabulary
- Pronunciation
- Listening
- Speaking
- Reading
- Writing
- Real-life communication

A learner often makes the mistake of studying only grammar.

Knowing grammar is useful, but grammar alone does not create fluency.

For example:

You may know this rule:

> Present perfect = have/has + past participle

But real communication requires you to understand when people actually use it:

> I have already finished the report.

> Have you ever visited Singapore?

> She has worked here for three years.

The best learning cycle is:

1. Understand the concept.
2. Read several examples.
3. Create your own examples.
4. Use the concept while speaking.
5. Use it while writing.
6. Listen for it in real English.
7. Review it later.

## A simple rule

Do not ask:

> "Do I know this grammar rule?"

Ask:

> "Can I understand it, use it, and recognize it naturally?"

# 2. English Learning Roadmap

A useful learning path is:

## Level A1 — Absolute Beginner

You should learn:

- Alphabet and sounds
- Basic greetings
- Numbers
- Days and months
- Common nouns
- Basic pronouns
- `am / is / are`
- Simple present
- Simple past
- Basic questions
- Basic prepositions
- Everyday vocabulary

Example:

> My name is Rahul.

> I live in Mumbai.

> I work in an office.

> I like coffee.

## Level A2 — Elementary

You should learn:

- Present continuous
- Past continuous
- Future forms
- Countable and uncountable nouns
- Comparatives
- Modals such as can, should, must
- Basic connectors
- More everyday vocabulary

Example:

> I am working from home today.

> I went to Pune last weekend.

> You should check the document again.

## Level B1 — Intermediate

You should learn:

- Present perfect
- Conditionals
- Passive voice
- Reported speech
- Relative clauses
- Phrasal verbs
- Better writing
- Longer conversations

Example:

> I have completed the development, but I haven't deployed it yet.

## Level B2 — Upper Intermediate

You should learn:

- Advanced connectors
- Natural expressions
- Complex sentence structures
- Business communication
- Presentation skills
- Debate and explanation
- Nuanced tense usage

Example:

> Although the issue initially appeared to be database-related, further investigation showed that the API was sending an invalid payload.

## Level C1/C2 — Advanced

You should develop:

- Precision
- Style
- Tone
- Argumentation
- Advanced vocabulary
- Natural pronunciation
- Idiomatic communication
- Academic/professional writing
- Fast listening comprehension

Example:

> The proposed solution is technically feasible; however, its long-term maintainability depends on how effectively we separate business logic from infrastructure concerns.

# 3. Basic Building Blocks of English

English sentences are built from words.

Words belong to different categories called **parts of speech**.

Example:

> The smart developer quickly fixed the issue.

Breakdown:

- `The` → article
- `smart` → adjective
- `developer` → noun
- `quickly` → adverb
- `fixed` → verb
- `the` → article
- `issue` → noun

Understanding these categories makes grammar much easier.


## From Words to Meaning

Knowing the name of a part of speech is useful only if it helps you understand how a sentence works.

Take:

> The new developer quickly fixed the production issue.

- `developer` tells us **who** performed the action.
- `fixed` tells us **what happened**.
- `issue` tells us **what received the action**.
- `new` describes the developer.
- `quickly` describes how the action happened.
- `the` points to a specific issue that speaker and listener can identify.

When you analyze a sentence, first find the **main verb**, then ask who or what performs it. This simple habit makes longer sentences easier to understand.

# 4. Parts of Speech

The major parts of speech are:

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Person, place, thing, idea | developer, Mumbai, laptop, freedom |
| Pronoun | Replaces a noun | he, she, it, they |
| Verb | Action or state | work, run, is, become |
| Adjective | Describes a noun | fast, useful, expensive |
| Adverb | Describes a verb/adjective/adverb | quickly, very, carefully |
| Preposition | Shows relationship | in, on, at, from, with |
| Conjunction | Connects ideas | and, but, because |
| Interjection | Expresses emotion | wow, oh, hey |
| Determiner | Specifies a noun | this, that, some, many |
| Article | A special determiner | a, an, the |


## How Parts of Speech Work Together

A word can sometimes belong to different categories depending on how it is used.

> I **work** every day.

`work` is a verb.

> I have a lot of **work** today.

`work` is a noun.

Another example:

> This is a **fast** car.

`fast` describes the noun `car`, so it is an adjective.

> The car moves **fast**.

Here `fast` describes how the car moves, so it functions as an adverb.

Do not identify a word only from its dictionary meaning. Look at its job in the sentence.

# 5. Sentence Structure

The most basic English sentence pattern is:

> Subject + Verb + Object

Example:

> I read books.

- Subject: I
- Verb: read
- Object: books

More examples:

> She writes code.

> They watch movies.

> We use Angular.

## Subject + Be + Complement

Example:

> He is tired.

> The server is down.

> They are developers.

## Subject + Verb

Some verbs do not need an object.

> The baby cried.

> The application crashed.

## Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object

> I sent him the file.

- I = subject
- sent = verb
- him = indirect object
- the file = direct object

## Sentence fragments

Incorrect:

> Because I was tired.

This is incomplete by itself.

Correct:

> I went home because I was tired.


## Complements and Objects

An **object** normally receives the action of a verb:

> She opened **the file**.

A **complement** completes the meaning of a subject or object, often after verbs such as `be`, `seem`, `become`, or `feel`:

> The file is **corrupted**.

`corrupted` describes the subject `file`.

### Why this matters

Some verbs need an object:

> She discussed **the problem**.

Some do not:

> The application crashed.

Adding an unnecessary object or preposition can create incorrect English. Learn common verbs together with their normal sentence patterns.

# 6. Nouns

A noun names a:

- Person
- Place
- Thing
- Animal
- Idea
- Activity

Examples:

- manager
- India
- computer
- dog
- honesty
- swimming

## Common nouns

General names:

> city, employee, laptop

## Proper nouns

Specific names:

> Mumbai, Microsoft, Monday

Proper nouns normally start with a capital letter.

## Countable nouns

You can count them.

> one book  
> two books

Use:

- a
- an
- many
- few
- several

Examples:

> I have a laptop.

> We received three invoices.

## Uncountable nouns

Normally not counted individually.

Examples:

- information
- advice
- furniture
- equipment
- water
- traffic
- knowledge

Incorrect:

> an advice

Correct:

> some advice

Incorrect:

> many informations

Correct:

> a lot of information

## Singular and plural

Common:

> book → books  
> car → cars

Words ending in `s`, `x`, `ch`, `sh` often take `-es`:

> box → boxes  
> watch → watches

Irregular:

> child → children  
> man → men  
> woman → women  
> person → people  
> mouse → mice

## Possessive nouns

Use `'s` for ownership:

> Rahul's laptop

> The manager's office

Plural ending in `s`:

> the employees' cafeteria


## Countability Changes Grammar

Whether a noun is countable affects articles, plurals, and quantity words.

Countable:

> a request  
> two requests  
> many requests  
> a few requests

Uncountable:

> some information  
> much information  
> a little information

Some nouns can be countable or uncountable with different meanings:

> I drank **coffee**.

`coffee` means the substance in general.

> We ordered **two coffees**.

In everyday speech, `two coffees` means two servings/cups.

Always learn the noun together with how it behaves in real sentences.

# 7. Pronouns

Pronouns replace nouns.

Instead of:

> Rahul said Rahul would send Rahul's report.

Use:

> Rahul said he would send his report.

## Subject pronouns

| Person | Pronoun |
|---|---|
| First-person singular | I |
| Second-person | you |
| Third-person masculine | he |
| Third-person feminine | she |
| Third-person thing | it |
| First-person plural | we |
| Third-person plural | they |

## Object pronouns

| Subject | Object |
|---|---|
| I | me |
| you | you |
| he | him |
| she | her |
| it | it |
| we | us |
| they | them |

Examples:

> She called me.

> I sent him the file.

## Possessive adjectives

> my, your, his, her, its, our, their

Example:

> This is my laptop.

## Possessive pronouns

> mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs

Example:

> This laptop is mine.

## Reflexive pronouns

> myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves

Example:

> I fixed it myself.


## Pronoun Reference Must Be Clear

A pronoun should point clearly to the noun it replaces.

Unclear:

> When Rahul spoke to Amit, **he** said the build was ready.

Who said it—Rahul or Amit?

Clear:

> Rahul told Amit that **the build was ready**.

or:

> Rahul said the build was ready when he spoke to Amit.

In technical and professional writing, repeat the noun when a pronoun could create ambiguity. Clarity is more important than avoiding repetition.

# 8. Articles: A, An, The

Articles are small but very important.

## A

Use `a` before a singular countable noun beginning with a consonant sound.

> a developer

> a laptop

> a university

Notice: `university` begins with a "yoo" sound, so we use `a`.

## An

Use `an` before a vowel sound.

> an apple

> an engineer

> an hour

`hour` begins with a vowel sound because the `h` is silent.

## The

Use `the` when the listener knows which specific thing you mean.

First mention:

> I bought a laptop.

Second mention:

> The laptop is very fast.

## No article

We often use no article when talking generally.

> Dogs are friendly.

> Water is important.

> Software development requires practice.


## Choosing an Article Step by Step

For a singular countable noun, ask:

1. Am I talking about one item in general or mentioning it for the first time?  
   Use `a` or `an`.

2. Does the listener know exactly which item I mean?  
   Use `the`.

Example:

> I received **an** email from a vendor. **The** email contains a revised quotation.

The first mention is non-specific to the listener. The second mention is now specific.

### Common mistake

Incorrect:

> I am developer.

Correct:

> I am **a** developer.

A singular countable profession normally needs an article.

# 9. Adjectives

Adjectives describe nouns.

Examples:

> a fast car

> an experienced developer

> a difficult problem

Adjectives do not normally change for singular/plural:

> a good employee

> good employees

## Adjective order

A common natural order is:

> opinion → size → age → shape → color → origin → material → purpose → noun

Example:

> a beautiful small old wooden table

You do not need to memorize the full order immediately. Learn common combinations through exposure.

## Adjectives after "be"

> The application is slow.

> The meeting was productive.


## Adjective vs Adverb

Adjectives normally describe nouns:

> The system is **slow**.

Adverbs often describe actions:

> The system responds **slowly**.

After linking verbs such as `be`, `seem`, `look`, `feel`, and `become`, an adjective is often needed:

> The result looks **good**.

Not normally:

> The result looks **well**.

`well` can be an adverb meaning "in a good way" and can also be an adjective meaning "healthy."

---

# 10. Adverbs

Adverbs often describe verbs.

> She speaks clearly.

> He works quickly.

They can also describe adjectives:

> extremely difficult

> very useful

Or other adverbs:

> incredibly quickly

## Frequency adverbs

Common:

- always
- usually
- often
- sometimes
- rarely
- never

With ordinary verbs:

> I usually work from home on Friday.

Before the main verb.

With `be`:

> I am usually busy in the morning.

After `be`.


## Adverb Position Can Change Emphasis

Compare:

> I **only** checked the logs.

This may mean I did nothing except check the logs.

> I checked **only the logs**.

This clearly means the logs were the only thing I checked.

Frequency adverbs also follow common patterns:

> She **usually works** from home.

> She **is usually** available after lunch.

When an adverb could modify more than one part of a sentence, place it close to the word or phrase you want it to describe.

---

# 11. Verbs

A verb expresses an action, condition, or state.

Examples:

> run

> work

> think

> understand

> be

## Main verbs

> I work in IT.

`work` is the main verb.

## Auxiliary/helping verbs

Examples:

- be
- have
- do

> I am working.

> I have finished.

> Do you know him?

## Regular verbs

Past form usually takes `-ed`.

> work → worked

> call → called

## Irregular verbs

> go → went → gone

> write → wrote → written

> take → took → taken

These must be learned gradually.

## Stative verbs

Some verbs usually describe states rather than actions.

Examples:

- know
- believe
- understand
- want
- need
- prefer
- belong

Normally:

> I know the answer.

Not usually:

> I am knowing the answer.


## Verb Patterns Matter

Learning a verb means learning more than its meaning.

For example:

> explain **something to someone**

> discuss **something**

> depend **on something**

> suggest **doing something**

> decide **to do something**

These patterns determine what can follow the verb.

### Transitive and intransitive use

A **transitive** verb takes an object:

> We resolved **the issue**.

An **intransitive** verb does not require one:

> The server crashed.

Some verbs can be both:

> The meeting started.

> The manager started the meeting.

Learning common patterns prevents errors that cannot be fixed by vocabulary knowledge alone.

# 12. The English Tense System

English tense learning becomes easier when you understand three ideas:

1. **Time:** past, present, future
2. **Aspect:** simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous
3. **Context:** why the speaker chose that form

---

## 12.1 Present Simple

### Structure

> Subject + base verb

For he/she/it:

> verb + s/es

Examples:

> I work in Mumbai.

> She works in finance.

### Uses

#### Habit

> I go to the gym every morning.

#### Fact

> Water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure.

#### Routine

> Our team meets every Monday.

#### Permanent or long-term situation

> She lives in Pune.

### Negative

> I do not work on Sunday.

> He does not work on Sunday.

Short:

> don't / doesn't

### Question

> Do you work here?

> Does she know you?

---

## 12.2 Present Continuous

### Structure

> am/is/are + verb-ing

Examples:

> I am studying English.

> They are working.

### Uses

#### Happening now

> I am speaking to my manager.

#### Temporary situation

> I am staying with my friend this week.

#### Changing situation

> The company is growing quickly.

#### Arranged future

> I am meeting the client tomorrow.

### Difference

> I work from home.  
Means this is normal.

> I am working from home today.  
Means today is temporary/current.

---

## 12.3 Present Perfect

### Structure

> have/has + past participle

Examples:

> I have finished the task.

> She has left the office.

### Uses

#### Past action connected to now

> I have lost my keys.

Meaning: I do not have them now.

#### Experience

> I have visited Dubai twice.

#### Unfinished time period

> I have attended three meetings today.

If today is not finished.

#### With `since` and `for`

> I have worked here for five years.

> I have worked here since 2021.

### Common words

- already
- yet
- just
- ever
- never
- recently

Examples:

> I have already sent the email.

> Have you finished yet?

---

## 12.4 Present Perfect Continuous

### Structure

> have/has been + verb-ing

Example:

> I have been studying for two hours.

### Use

An activity started in the past and continues now, or has just stopped with a visible result.

> It has been raining all morning.

> I am tired because I have been working all day.

---

## 12.5 Past Simple

### Structure

> verb in past form

Examples:

> I worked yesterday.

> She went home.

### Uses

Completed action in the past:

> I called him yesterday.

Series of completed actions:

> I opened the file, checked the values, and sent the report.

### Negative

> I did not call him.

Not:

> I did not called him.

After `did`, use the base verb.

### Question

> Did you call him?

---

## 12.6 Past Continuous

### Structure

> was/were + verb-ing

Example:

> I was working at 8 PM.

### Uses

Activity in progress at a past time:

> She was sleeping when I called.

Two simultaneous actions:

> I was cooking while he was watching TV.

Background action:

> It was raining when we left the office.

---

## 12.7 Past Perfect

### Structure

> had + past participle

Use it when one past event happened before another past event.

> The train had left before we reached the station.

Timeline:

1. Train left.
2. We reached station.

Another example:

> I realized that I had forgotten my wallet.

---

## 12.8 Past Perfect Continuous

### Structure

> had been + verb-ing

Example:

> I had been waiting for an hour before the bus arrived.

It emphasizes duration before another past event.

---

## 12.9 Future with "Will"

### Structure

> will + base verb

Uses:

#### Instant decision

> I'll call him now.

#### Prediction

> I think it will rain.

#### Promise

> I will send the file today.

---

## 12.10 "Going to"

### Structure

> am/is/are going to + base verb

Uses:

#### Plan

> I am going to start a new course.

#### Prediction with evidence

> Look at those clouds. It is going to rain.

---

## 12.11 Present Continuous for Future Arrangements

> I am meeting the client at 3 PM tomorrow.

This sounds like the arrangement is already planned.

---

## 12.12 Future Continuous

> will be + verb-ing

Example:

> I will be working at 10 AM tomorrow.

Use for an activity in progress at a future time.

---

## 12.13 Future Perfect

> will have + past participle

Example:

> By Friday, I will have completed the migration.

---

## 12.14 Future Perfect Continuous

> will have been + verb-ing

Example:

> By December, I will have been working here for five years.

---

## Tense Comparison

| Situation | Example |
|---|---|
| Routine | I work every day. |
| Happening now | I am working now. |
| Completed with present relevance | I have finished the work. |
| Ongoing since past | I have been working for three hours. |
| Completed past | I worked yesterday. |
| Ongoing at past moment | I was working at 8 PM. |
| Earlier past | I had worked there before joining this company. |
| Prediction | I will work tomorrow. |
| Plan | I am going to work tomorrow. |

# 13. Modal Verbs

Modal verbs express ability, possibility, permission, obligation, advice, etc.

Common modals:

- can
- could
- may
- might
- must
- should
- would
- shall

After a modal, use the base form:

> can go

Not:

> can goes

## Can

Ability:

> I can swim.

Permission:

> Can I use your laptop?

## Could

Past ability:

> I could swim when I was five.

Polite request:

> Could you please send the file?

Possibility:

> It could fail if the configuration is wrong.

## May

Formal permission:

> May I come in?

Possibility:

> It may rain later.

## Might

Weaker possibility:

> He might join the meeting.

## Must

Strong obligation:

> You must wear an ID card.

Strong logical conclusion:

> The server is responding slowly. It must be overloaded.

## Should

Advice:

> You should back up the database.

Expectation:

> The deployment should finish soon.

## Would

Polite request:

> Would you help me?

Hypothetical:

> I would travel more if I had time.

Past habit:

> When I was young, I would play cricket every evening.


## Modal Meaning Depends on Context

Modals do not map to one fixed meaning.

Compare:

> You **must** wear an ID card.

Strong obligation.

> He left his laptop here, so he **must** be nearby.

Strong logical conclusion.

> You **could** restart the service.

Possible suggestion.

> The service **could** fail under heavy load.

Possibility.

### Modal + perfect form

For past possibilities, deductions, or regrets, English often uses:

> modal + have + past participle

Examples:

> The deployment **may have failed** because of the certificate.

> We **should have tested** the rollback earlier.

This structure talks about a past situation while expressing the speaker's judgment about it.

# 14. Subject-Verb Agreement

The verb must agree with the subject.

> He works.

> They work.

Common mistake:

> He work in IT.

Correct:

> He works in IT.

## With `be`

> I am

> He is

> They are

## Tricky cases

> Everyone is ready.

`Everyone` is grammatically singular.

> The team is ready.

In American English, collective nouns are often treated as singular. British English may sometimes use plural agreement depending on meaning.

> The list of items is on the table.

The subject is `list`, not `items`.


## Finding the Real Subject

Do not let a nearby plural noun control the verb if it is not the subject.

> The **list** of failed transactions **is** attached.

The subject is `list`, not `transactions`.

> The **results** of the test **are** attached.

The subject is `results`.

### Indefinite pronouns

Words such as `everyone`, `someone`, `anyone`, `each`, and `everybody` are normally singular in standard grammar:

> Everyone **is** ready.

> Each of the files **has** a unique ID.

In conversation, singular `they` is common and standard for referring to a person whose gender is unknown or irrelevant:

> Everyone should bring **their** ID.

# 16. Conjunctions and Connectors

Connectors make your speech and writing more logical.

## Addition

- and
- also
- furthermore
- moreover
- in addition

Example:

> The system is fast and reliable.

## Contrast

- but
- however
- although
- even though
- whereas
- while

> The solution is cheap, but it is difficult to maintain.

> Although the task was difficult, we completed it.

## Reason

- because
- since
- as
- due to
- because of

> We postponed the deployment because the server was unavailable.

## Result

- so
- therefore
- thus
- consequently
- as a result

> The server was unavailable, so we postponed the deployment.

## Example

- for example
- for instance
- such as

## Sequence

- first
- next
- then
- after that
- finally


## Connector Grammar

Some connectors join clauses; others are followed by noun phrases.

Clause:

> We postponed the release **because the test failed**.

Noun phrase:

> We postponed the release **because of the test failure**.

Clause:

> **Although the system was slow**, it remained available.

Sentence connector:

> The system was slow. **However,** it remained available.

`although` and `however` are not interchangeable in sentence structure even though both express contrast.

# 17. Questions

## Yes/No questions with `be`

Statement:

> You are ready.

Question:

> Are you ready?

## With ordinary verbs

Use `do/does/did`.

> Do you work here?

> Does he work here?

> Did they finish?

## WH questions

- what
- where
- when
- why
- who
- which
- whose
- how

Examples:

> Where do you live?

> Why did the application fail?

> How does this function work?

## Question word as subject

> Who called you?

No `did` is required because `who` is the subject.


## Question Word Order

A common question pattern is:

> question word + auxiliary + subject + main verb

> Where **do you work**?

> Why **did the service fail**?

With `be`, move the verb before the subject:

> Where **is the file**?

When the question word itself is the subject, do not add `do/does/did`:

> Who called you?

Not:

> Who did call you?

The form `Who did call you?` is possible only in special emphatic contexts, not as the normal neutral question.

# 18. Negatives

## Be

> I am not tired.

> She is not available.

## Present simple

> I do not know.

> He does not know.

## Past

> I did not go.

## Present perfect

> I have not finished.

## Modal

> I cannot attend.

> You should not do that.


## Negatives and Scope

Where you place the negative can affect meaning.

> I **don't think** the deployment is ready.

Natural way to express a negative opinion.

> I think the deployment is **not ready**.

Also correct, with slightly more direct focus on `not ready`.

### Avoid accidental double negatives

Standard:

> We didn't receive **any** response.

Not standard for the intended negative meaning:

> We didn't receive **no** response.

Some dialects use multiple negatives systematically, but standard formal English normally uses one grammatical negative for this meaning.

# 19. Active and Passive Voice

## Active

> The developer fixed the bug.

Focus: developer.

## Passive

> The bug was fixed by the developer.

Focus: bug.

### Structure

> be + past participle

Examples:

> The email was sent yesterday.

> The issue has been resolved.

> The application will be deployed tomorrow.

Passive voice is common in:

- formal writing
- technical documentation
- scientific writing
- situations where the action matters more than the actor

Use active voice when possible for clearer everyday communication.


## When Passive Voice Is Useful

Use passive voice when the action or result matters more than the actor:

> The database was restored at 2:15 PM.

This is useful in incident timelines.

Use active voice when ownership matters:

> The database team restored the database at 2:15 PM.

### Do not use passive voice to hide responsibility unnecessarily

Vague:

> A mistake was made.

Clearer when the actor matters:

> The deployment team selected the wrong configuration file.

Choose the voice that makes the important information easiest to understand.

# 20. Direct and Reported Speech

## Direct speech

> She said, "I am tired."

## Reported speech

> She said that she was tired.

Common changes after a past reporting verb:

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| am/is | was |
| are | were |
| have | had |
| will | would |
| can | could |
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next day |
| yesterday | the previous day |

Example:

Direct:

> He said, "I will call you tomorrow."

Reported:

> He said that he would call me the next day.

Not every situation requires backshifting, especially if the information is still true.


## Reporting Changes More Than Pronouns

When reporting speech, tense, time expressions, and pronouns may change depending on the reporting context.

Direct:

> Priya said, "I will send the report tomorrow."

Reported later:

> Priya said that she would send the report the next day.

However, tense backshift is not always required when the information is still true.

> The trainer said that water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure.

The fact remains true, so keeping the present tense is natural.

# 21. Conditionals

Conditionals describe results that depend on conditions.

## Zero Conditional

General truth:

> If you heat ice, it melts.

Structure:

> if + present, present

## First Conditional

Real future possibility:

> If it rains, we will stay home.

## Second Conditional

Hypothetical/unlikely present or future:

> If I had more time, I would learn Spanish.

## Third Conditional

Imaginary past:

> If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam.

## Mixed Conditional

Past condition with present result:

> If I had accepted that job, I would be living in Bangalore now.


## Choosing a Conditional

Use the conditional that matches the time and reality of the situation.

- **Zero:** general result or rule.  
  > If you heat ice, it melts.

- **First:** realistic future possibility.  
  > If testing passes, we will deploy tonight.

- **Second:** hypothetical or unlikely present/future.  
  > If I had more time, I would study another language.

- **Third:** unreal past.  
  > If we had backed up the file, we would not have lost the data.

Do not choose a conditional because it sounds more advanced. Choose it because it matches the meaning.

# 22. Gerunds and Infinitives

## Gerund

Verb + `-ing` used like a noun.

> Swimming is good exercise.

> I enjoy reading.

## Infinitive

`to + base verb`

> I want to learn English.

Some verbs commonly take gerunds:

- enjoy
- avoid
- consider
- finish
- suggest

> I enjoy learning.

Some commonly take infinitives:

- want
- need
- plan
- decide
- hope

> I plan to travel.

Some change meaning:

> I stopped smoking.  
I quit the habit.

> I stopped to smoke.  
I stopped another activity in order to smoke.


## Verb Meaning Can Change the Pattern

Some verbs can take both a gerund and an infinitive but with a different meaning.

> I remembered **to send** the email.

I did not forget the required action.

> I remember **sending** the email.

I have a memory of the completed action.

Another pair:

> Try **restarting** the service.

Experiment with restarting as a possible solution.

> Try **to restart** the service.

Make an effort to restart it.

Because patterns can change meaning, learn important verbs with complete example sentences.

# 23. Relative Clauses

Relative clauses add information about a noun.

## Who

For people:

> The developer who fixed the issue is on leave.

## Which

For things:

> The laptop which I bought is fast.

## That

For people or things in many defining clauses:

> The file that you sent is corrupted.

## Whose

Possession:

> The employee whose laptop was stolen reported the incident.

## Where

Place:

> This is the office where I worked.


## Defining vs Non-Defining Relative Clauses

A **defining** relative clause identifies which person or thing you mean:

> The developer **who handles payments** is on leave.

The clause is necessary to identify the developer.

A **non-defining** relative clause adds extra information:

> Priya, **who handles payments**, is on leave.

The name already identifies the person, so the clause is additional information and is separated by commas.

`that` is common in defining clauses but is not normally used in non-defining clauses:

> The API, **which** was updated yesterday, is stable.

Not normally:

> The API, **that** was updated yesterday, is stable.

> fast → fastest

# 25. Punctuation and Capitalization

## Full stop / period

Ends a statement.

> The task is complete.

## Comma

Separates items or clauses.

> We bought monitors, keyboards, and laptops.

After introductory phrases:

> After the meeting, I sent the report.

## Question mark

> Are you available?

## Exclamation mark

> Great job!

Use sparingly in professional communication.

## Colon

Introduces a list or explanation.

> We need three things: time, data, and approval.

## Semicolon

Connects closely related independent clauses.

> The API is working; the frontend is still failing.

## Apostrophe

Possession:

> Rahul's laptop

Contraction:

> don't = do not

## Capitalization

Capitalize:

- first word of a sentence
- names
- countries
- languages
- days
- months
- official titles when used as names in certain contexts

Examples:

> I speak English.

> We will meet on Monday.


## Punctuation Changes Meaning

Compare:

> Let's eat, Grandma.

The comma shows that you are speaking to Grandma.

> Let's eat Grandma.

Without the comma, the sentence humorously suggests that Grandma is the food.

### Comma splices

Incorrect in standard formal writing:

> The API failed, we restarted the service.

Better:

> The API failed, so we restarted the service.

or:

> The API failed. We restarted the service.

or:

> The API failed; we restarted the service.

Use punctuation to show the grammatical relationship between ideas, not only to create pauses.

# 27. Pronunciation

Perfect accent is not required.

The real goals are:

- clarity
- correct sounds
- understandable stress
- natural rhythm
- confidence

## 27.1 Sounds vs spelling

English spelling is not perfectly phonetic.

Examples:

> though

> through

> tough

These look similar but sound different.

## 27.2 Word stress

One syllable is often stronger.

Examples:

> PREsent — noun

> preSENT — verb

> REcord — noun

> reCORD — verb

## 27.3 Sentence stress

Important content words are usually stressed more:

> I **SENT** the **REPORT** to the **CLIENT**.

Function words are often weaker:

- the
- a
- to
- of
- and

## 27.4 Connected speech

Natural English is not spoken word-by-word.

Example:

> What are you doing?

May sound more like:

> Whaddaya doing?

You do not need to copy every reduction, but you should learn to recognize them.

## 27.5 Important sound distinctions

Practice pairs:

> ship / sheep

> live / leave

> full / fool

> fan / van

> rice / rise

> think / sink

## 27.6 `th`

Voiceless:

> think, three, thank

Voiced:

> this, that, those

## 27.7 Final consonants

Do not drop important final sounds.

Compare:

> work

> worked

> works

The ending changes grammar and meaning.

## Pronunciation practice method

1. Listen to a short sentence.
2. Pause.
3. Repeat exactly.
4. Record yourself.
5. Compare.
6. Repeat 5–10 times.

This method is called **shadowing** when you speak along with or immediately after the audio.

# 29. Speaking Skills

Fluency does not mean speaking extremely fast.

Fluency means:

- expressing ideas without excessive stopping
- using understandable grammar
- choosing suitable vocabulary
- recovering when you do not know a word

## Use simple English first

Instead of:

> Due to the fact that the application encountered an unexpected technical difficulty...

Say:

> The application failed because of an unexpected issue.

## Conversation framework

A good answer can use:

> Answer + Reason + Example + Follow-up

Question:

> Do you like working from home?

Answer:

> Yes, I do. I save commuting time, so I can start work with more energy. For example, I usually use that extra time to exercise. How about you?

## When you forget a word

Do not stop.

Describe it:

> It is a tool that we use to...

> It is similar to...

> I don't remember the exact word, but...

This is an important fluency skill called **circumlocution**.

## Useful hesitation phrases

Natural:

> Let me think.

> That's a good question.

> As far as I know...

> From my experience...

Avoid overusing:

> um...

> actually...

> basically...

> like...


## Build Fluency With Chunks

A **chunk** is a group of words commonly used together.

Examples:

> As far as I know...

> The main reason is...

> Could you clarify what you mean by...?

> One possible option is...

Chunks reduce the amount of grammar you must assemble word by word while speaking.

### Accuracy vs fluency practice

During **accuracy practice**, slow down and correct grammar.

During **fluency practice**, keep communicating and correct only errors that block meaning. Mixing both goals every second can make speech hesitant.

# 30. Reading Skills

Reading improves:

- vocabulary
- grammar intuition
- writing
- sentence structure
- general comprehension

## Skimming

Read quickly to understand the main idea.

Use when:

- checking an article
- reading a long email
- reviewing documentation

## Scanning

Search for specific information.

Example:

Find:

- invoice number
- date
- deadline
- error code

## Intensive reading

Read carefully and analyze details.

Useful for:

- contracts
- technical documentation
- learning material

## Extensive reading

Read large amounts for general understanding and enjoyment.

Useful for fluency.


## Match the Reading Method to the Task

Use **skimming** when you need the overall message.

Use **scanning** when you need one fact.

Use **intensive reading** when every detail may matter.

Use **extensive reading** when your goal is exposure and fluency.

### Example

For a long technical incident report:

1. Skim the headings to understand the structure.
2. Scan for the incident time and affected service.
3. Read the root-cause section intensively.
4. Note unfamiliar terms that are important to your work.

Efficient readers change method instead of reading every document at the same speed.

# 31. Writing Skills

Good writing is:

- clear
- organized
- concise
- grammatically correct
- appropriate for the audience

## Basic paragraph structure

A paragraph often contains:

1. Topic sentence
2. Explanation
3. Example
4. Conclusion/link

Example:

> Remote work can improve productivity for some employees. It reduces commuting time and allows people to create a personalized work environment. For example, an employee who normally spends two hours commuting may use that time for focused work or exercise. However, remote work is most effective when communication and expectations are clear.

## Avoid overly long sentences

Weak:

> I checked the server and there was an error and then I contacted the infrastructure team and they checked the configuration and they said the certificate was expired so we renewed it and then the application started working.

Better:

> I checked the server and found a certificate error. I contacted the infrastructure team, who confirmed that the certificate had expired. After we renewed it, the application started working.

## Use specific verbs

Weak:

> We did a change.

Better:

> We updated the configuration.

Weak:

> We did an investigation.

Better:

> We investigated the issue.


## Editing Is Part of Writing

A strong first draft is not required. Good writing often comes from revision.

Use three passes:

1. **Meaning:** Is the message complete and logically organized?
2. **Language:** Are grammar and word choice clear?
3. **Polish:** Can unnecessary words be removed?

Example:

Draft:

> Due to the fact that the server was not available, therefore we were unable to proceed with testing.

Edited:

> Because the server was unavailable, we could not proceed with testing.

The edited version removes duplication: `due to the fact that` and `therefore` were both trying to express the same cause-result relationship.

# 33. Professional and Business English

## Asking for an update

> Could you please share an update on this issue?

More direct:

> Do we have any update on this issue?

## Asking for help

> Could you please help me with this?

Better when context matters:

> Could you please help us identify the correct point of contact for this application?

## Escalating politely

> This issue is currently blocking production deployment. Could you please prioritize it?

## Disagreeing professionally

Instead of:

> You are wrong.

Say:

> I see it differently.

> I may be missing something, but my understanding is that...

> I understand your point; however, the current implementation behaves differently.

## Suggesting

> I suggest that we validate the data before posting it.

> We could consider adding a fallback mechanism.

> One option would be to handle this at the API layer.

## Clarifying

> Just to confirm, should this apply to all users or only administrators?

> When you say "completed," do you mean development is complete or production deployment is complete?


## Professional English Is Outcome-Oriented

A strong workplace message usually contains enough information to support action.

Compare:

> Need help with server.

Better:

> The UAT server has been unavailable since 10:30 AM, which is blocking invoice testing. Could the infrastructure team please check the service and share an update by 11:30 AM?

The second message states:

- the system
- the current state
- the start time
- the impact
- the requested owner/action
- the requested update time

Use this level of detail when the situation is operationally important.

1. Greeting
2. Reason for writing
3. Important details
4. Required action
5. Closing

Weak:

> Issue

Better:

> Assistance Required: Application Ownership Details

Weak:

> Help

Better:

> Support Required for Production Deployment

## Chat vs email

Chat can be shorter:

> Hi Ravi, could you please check the deployment status when you get a chance?

Email usually needs more context.


## Email vs Chat: Choose Deliberately

Use **email** when you need:

- a formal request or approval
- a decision trail
- a detailed summary
- information for several stakeholders
- an attachment or structured context

Use **chat** when you need:

- a quick question
- short coordination
- rapid clarification
- a brief status update

If an important decision happens in chat or on a call, summarize it in the system of record used by your organization.

### Attachment check

Before sending:

> Please find the report attached.

confirm that the correct file is actually attached and that the version is the intended one.

# 35. Presentation and Meeting English

## Opening a presentation

> Good morning, everyone. Today I'll walk you through the current status of the project and the next steps.

> Let's move on to the next point.

> Now let's look at the architecture.

## Referring to a slide

> As you can see on this slide...

> This chart shows...

> Please feel free to ask questions as we go.

> Are there any questions before I continue?

> I understand the concern. My suggestion would be to test both options before making a final decision.

> Sorry to interrupt, but could I clarify one point?

## Bringing discussion back

> That's useful context. To bring us back to the main issue...

## Ending

> To summarize, we have three action items...


## A Presentation Needs Verbal Structure

Slides show information; your language tells the audience how the information connects.

Use:

> First, I'll explain the current problem.

> The key point on this slide is...

> This result is important because...

> To summarize...

> Average response time decreased from 2.4 seconds to 1.6 seconds after the optimization.

Do not merely read every word from the slide.

# 36. Interview English

## Tell me about yourself

Useful structure:

1. Current role
2. Experience
3. Main skills
4. Major achievements
5. What you want next

Example:

> I'm a full-stack developer with several years of experience building and maintaining enterprise applications. I mainly work with frontend frameworks, APIs, databases, and business process automation. In my current role, I have worked on modules involving invoice processing, workflow automation, reporting, and system integrations. I'm now looking for a role where I can work on larger-scale systems and continue growing in architecture and cloud technologies.

## Strength

> One of my strengths is problem-solving. When I encounter an issue, I try to identify the root cause rather than only fixing the visible symptom.

## Weakness

Avoid fake weaknesses.

Good structure:

> Earlier, I sometimes spent too much time trying to perfect a solution before sharing progress. I've improved this by communicating earlier and getting feedback sooner.

## Explain a technical problem

Use:

> Problem → Investigation → Solution → Result


## Interview Answers Need Evidence

Avoid unsupported claims:

> I am very good at problem-solving.

Better:

> One of my strengths is problem-solving. In a recent incident, I traced repeated invoice failures to a validation mismatch, corrected the mapping, and added a check to prevent the same invalid data from reaching the database.

Use real details you can explain. Do not invent metrics, technologies, or responsibilities to make an answer sound stronger.

# 37. English for Software and IT Professionals

## Describing a bug

Weak:

> It is not working.

Better:

> The application returns a 500 error when the user submits the form.

Better:

> The issue occurs only when the invoice contains more than 100 line items. The API throws a timeout after approximately 30 seconds.

## Explaining root cause

> The root cause was an expired SSL certificate.

> The issue was caused by an incorrect database connection string.

> The frontend was sending a string instead of a numeric value.

## Explaining resolution

> We updated the configuration and restarted the application pool.

> We added validation to prevent invalid values from reaching the database.

## Deployment language

> The build has been deployed to UAT.

> Production deployment is scheduled for Friday.

> The rollback was completed successfully.

## Code review language

> Could we move this logic into a separate service?

> This works, but we may want to handle the null case explicitly.

> Consider renaming this variable to make its purpose clearer.

> We should add a test for this edge case.

## Incident call language

> We are currently investigating the issue.

> We have identified the root cause.

> A temporary workaround is in place.

> The permanent fix is being tested.

> The service has been restored.


## Technical Communication Should Separate Fact From Hypothesis

Confirmed fact:

> The API returns HTTP 500 for invoices with more than 100 line items.

Hypothesis:

> The timeout may be related to processing time in the validation service.

Confirmed root cause:

> Log analysis confirmed that the validation query exceeded the configured request timeout.

This distinction matters during incidents. Saying "root cause" too early can send the team in the wrong direction.

### Explain to the audience

Technical audience:

> The API returned 500 after the database command timeout expired.

Business audience:

> Large invoices are currently failing during processing. The team has identified the failing step and is testing a correction.

Both statements can be accurate while using different levels of technical detail.

# 38. Common Mistakes and Corrections

These are especially common among learners whose first language has a very different grammar system.

| Incorrect | Correct | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I am having a laptop. | I have a laptop. | `have` for possession is usually stative. |
| I didn't went. | I didn't go. | Use base verb after `did`. |
| He don't know. | He doesn't know. | Third-person singular. |
| She is working here since 2022. | She has been working here since 2022. | Started in past and continues now. |
| I am agree. | I agree. | `agree` is a verb. |
| Discuss about this. | Discuss this. | `discuss` normally takes a direct object. |
| Revert back to me. | Reply to me / Get back to me. | `revert` is often misused in Indian business English. |
| Do the needful. | Please take the necessary action. | More natural international business English. |
| Kindly revert. | Please reply. | More natural in global communication. |
| Myself Rahul. | I'm Rahul. | Natural introduction. |
| He is my cousin brother. | He is my cousin. | English normally uses `cousin`. |
| I have one doubt. | I have a question. | `question` is more natural in this context. |
| Today morning | This morning | Standard expression. |
| Yesterday night | Last night | Standard expression. |
| Out of station | Out of town / away | More widely understood. |
| I passed out in 2020. | I graduated in 2020. | `pass out` usually means lose consciousness. |
| I am working in this company from 5 years. | I have been working at this company for five years. | `for` + duration. |
| Please explain me. | Please explain it to me. | `explain` uses `to` before person. |
| He told that... | He said that... / He told me that... | `tell` normally needs an object. |
| One of my friend | One of my friends | Use plural after `one of`. |
| More better | Better | Comparative already included. |
| Return back | Return | `back` is redundant. |
| Repeat again | Repeat | Usually redundant. |
| Order for food | Order food | Direct object. |
| I am understanding. | I understand. | Usually stative. |

# 39. Natural English: Collocations, Phrasal Verbs, and Idioms

## 39.1 Collocations

A collocation is a combination of words that commonly occur together.

Natural:

> make a decision

Not usually:

> do a decision

Natural:

> take responsibility

> meet a deadline

> solve a problem

> raise a concern

> conduct an investigation

> heavy traffic

> strong coffee

> highly recommended

Learning collocations makes your English sound much more natural.

---

## 39.2 Phrasal Verbs

A phrasal verb combines a verb with a particle.

### Look into

Meaning: investigate

> I'll look into the issue.

### Find out

Meaning: discover

> We need to find out why the service stopped.

### Set up

Meaning: configure/arrange

> I set up the development environment.

### Bring up

Meaning: mention

> She brought up an important concern.

### Follow up

Meaning: contact again or continue checking

> I'll follow up with the infrastructure team.

### Roll back

Meaning: restore a previous version

> We rolled back the deployment.

### Carry out

Meaning: perform

> We carried out a security review.

---

## 39.3 Idioms

Idioms should be learned carefully. Do not force them into every conversation.

### On the same page

Meaning: have the same understanding.

> Let's make sure we're all on the same page.

### Back to square one

Meaning: return to the beginning.

> The configuration failed, so we're back to square one.

### Learn the ropes

Meaning: learn how something works.

> It took me a few weeks to learn the ropes.

### In the long run

Meaning: over a long period.

> Automation will save time in the long run.

# 40. Formal vs Informal English

## Informal

> Can you send me the file?

## Neutral professional

> Could you please send me the file?

## More formal

> Could you please share the requested file at your earliest convenience?

Do not make everything overly formal.

Overly formal:

> Kindly do the needful at the earliest.

Natural professional:

> Please take the necessary action when possible.

## Contractions

Informal/neutral:

> don't, can't, we're, I'll

Formal reports may use fewer contractions:

> do not, cannot, we are


## Formal Does Not Mean Complicated

Good formal English is controlled and precise, not old-fashioned.

Overly formal:

> We hereby request you to kindly furnish the aforementioned details at the earliest.

Clear professional English:

> Please share the requested details by Friday.

### Match formality to risk

A quick teammate question may use contractions:

> Can you check this when you're free?

A policy or audit statement may use more explicit wording:

> Access must be reviewed and approved before production deployment.

Choose language based on audience, purpose, and consequence.

# 41. Thinking in English

Translation slows speaking.

Instead of:

1. Think in your first language.
2. Translate into English.
3. Speak.

Train yourself to:

1. Think in simple English.
2. Speak directly.

Start with simple thoughts:

> I am hungry.

> I need to finish this task.

> The train is crowded.

> I should call him later.

## Describe your environment

> There is a laptop on my desk.

> I can hear traffic outside.

> I am checking my email.

This builds automaticity.


## Thinking in English Starts With Small Units

Do not force yourself to think in complex English immediately.

Begin with labels and short observations:

> crowded train

> delayed meeting

> need to reply

Then expand:

> The train is crowded.

> The meeting has been delayed by 30 minutes.

> I need to reply before lunch.

This gradually reduces translation because the English phrase becomes connected directly to the situation or idea.

# 42. Fluency Strategy

Fluency requires **automatic recall**.

You may know 10,000 words but still speak slowly if you cannot retrieve them quickly.

## Build sentence patterns

Instead of memorizing isolated words, memorize useful structures.

### Pattern

> I would like to...

Examples:

> I would like to clarify one point.

> I would like to request access.

> I would like to understand the process.

### Pattern

> The reason is that...

> The reason is that the API depends on an external service.

### Pattern

> From my understanding...

> From my understanding, this change only applies to new users.

### Pattern

> It depends on...

> It depends on the invoice type.

## Fluency drill

Choose one topic and speak for:

- 30 seconds
- 60 seconds
- 2 minutes
- 5 minutes

Record yourself.

Repeat the same topic the next day.

You will notice improvement because your brain starts retrieving useful phrases faster.


## Fluency Is Fast Access to Useful Language

You do not need to speak quickly. You need to retrieve useful language without excessive searching.

A practical drill:

1. Choose one familiar topic.
2. Speak for two minutes.
3. Listen to the recording.
4. Write down three places where you paused or used unclear wording.
5. Prepare better phrases.
6. Speak on the same topic again.

Example improvement:

First attempt:

> The issue... it was... because server problem...

Improved chunk:

> The issue occurred because the application could not connect to the server.

Repeated retrieval turns useful patterns into automatic language.

# 50. Core Learning Advice

English is not a subject that you "finish."

It is a skill that becomes stronger through repeated use.

You do not need perfect grammar before speaking.

You do not need an American or British accent to communicate well.

You do not need to know every word.

Focus on:

> clarity + consistency + practice + feedback

A strong learner does four things regularly:

1. **Consumes English**  
   Read and listen.

2. **Produces English**  
   Speak and write.

3. **Reviews mistakes**  
   Notice repeated errors.

4. **Uses English in real situations**  
   Meetings, chats, emails, calls, presentations, interviews, hobbies, and everyday life.

The goal is not:

> "I never make mistakes."

The better goal is:

> "I can communicate clearly, naturally, and confidently—and I keep improving."

<a id="chapter-51"></a>
# 51. Determiners and Quantifiers

Determiners come before nouns and help tell us **which one, whose, how many, or how much**.

Common determiners include:

- a, an, the
- this, that, these, those
- my, your, his, her, our, their
- some, any
- much, many
- few, little
- each, every
- either, neither
- enough
- all, both

## This / That / These / Those

Near:

> this laptop

> these files

Far:

> that building

> those people

## Some

Usually used in positive statements:

> I need some information.

It is also common in offers and requests when the speaker expects a positive response:

> Would you like some coffee?

> Could I have some water?

## Any

Common in negatives and questions:

> I don't have any questions.

> Do you have any questions?

## Many vs Much

`many` → countable nouns

> many users

`much` → uncountable nouns

> much time

In everyday positive statements, `a lot of` often sounds more natural:

> We have a lot of work.

## Few / A Few

> few people

means almost none.

> a few people

means some.

Compare:

> Few employees understood the process.

This sounds negative.

> A few employees understood the process.

Some employees did.

## Little / A Little

For uncountable nouns.

> We have little time.

Almost no time.

> We have a little time.

Some time is available.

## Each vs Every

`each` focuses on individuals:

> Each employee received a card.

`every` views all members of a group:

> Every employee must complete the training.

## Both / Either / Neither

Two items:

> Both options are valid.

> You can choose either option.

> Neither option works.

<a id="chapter-52"></a>
# 52. Clause Types

A **clause** is a group of words containing a subject and a verb.

## Independent Clause

Can stand alone:

> The deployment succeeded.

## Dependent Clause

Cannot normally stand alone:

> because the configuration was correct

Combined:

> The deployment succeeded because the configuration was correct.

## Noun Clause

Acts like a noun:

> I know **that the server is down**.

> What he said was correct.

## Adjective/Relative Clause

Describes a noun:

> The developer **who joined yesterday** is working with us.

## Adverb Clause

Gives information about time, reason, condition, contrast, etc.

> I'll call you **when I reach home**.

> We stopped the deployment **because the test failed**.

<a id="chapter-53"></a>
# 53. Sentence Types

## Simple Sentence

One independent clause:

> The server failed.

## Compound Sentence

Two independent clauses joined together:

> The server failed, so we restarted it.

## Complex Sentence

Independent + dependent clause:

> We restarted the server because it had stopped responding.

## Compound-Complex Sentence

Multiple independent clauses plus at least one dependent clause:

> We restarted the server because it had stopped responding, and the application became available again.

Strong English writing uses a healthy mix of sentence lengths and structures.

# 54. Participles

Participles are verb forms that can be used in several grammatical structures.

## Present Participle

Usually `-ing`:

> running

> working

> developing

Used in continuous tenses:

> She is working.

Used like an adjective:

> an interesting book

## Past Participle

Examples:

> worked

> written

> broken

Used in perfect tenses:

> I have finished.

Used in passive voice:

> The issue was resolved.

Used like an adjective:

> a broken laptop

## Participial Phrase

> Having completed the test, we deployed the application.

This means:

> After we completed the test, we deployed the application.

Use advanced participial phrases carefully. Clarity is more important than sounding sophisticated.

<a id="chapter-58"></a>
# 58. Ellipsis and Substitution

English often removes repeated information.

Full:

> I can attend the meeting, and she can attend the meeting too.

Natural:

> I can attend the meeting, and she can too.

Substitution:

> I prefer the newer version to the old one.

`one` replaces `version`.


## Common Forms

Ellipsis often appears after auxiliaries:

> I can attend, and Priya can too.

The omitted words are understood from context.

Substitution avoids repeating a noun:

> The old server is slower than the new **one**.

For plural nouns:

> These logs are clearer than the previous **ones**.

Use ellipsis only when the missing meaning is obvious. If removing words creates ambiguity, repeat the necessary information.

<a id="chapter-65"></a>
# 65. Pronunciation of -ED Endings

Regular past verbs ending in `-ed` have three common pronunciations.

## `/t/`

After certain voiceless sounds:

> worked

> watched

## `/d/`

After many voiced sounds:

> played

> cleaned

## `/ɪd/` or `/əd/`

After `t` or `d` sounds:

> wanted

> needed

This is important because learners often pronounce every `-ed` as a separate syllable.


## The Sound Depends on the Final Sound, Not the Final Letter

Use `/t/` after voiceless sounds such as `/p/`, `/k/`, `/f/`, `/s/`, `/ʃ/`, and `/tʃ/`:

> stopped, worked, laughed, missed, washed, watched

Use `/d/` after vowels and voiced sounds other than `/d/`:

> played, cleaned, called, used

Use an extra syllable `/ɪd/` or `/əd/` after `/t/` or `/d/`:

> wanted, needed

The rule is about pronunciation. The spelling remains `-ed`.

<a id="chapter-66"></a>
# 66. Pronunciation of Plural and Third-Person -S

`-s` can sound like:

## `/s/`

> works

> stops

## `/z/`

> plays

> runs

## `/ɪz/`

> watches

> uses

Correct endings improve both pronunciation and grammar clarity.


## How to Choose the Correct `-s` Sound

The spelling is the same, but the sound depends on the final sound of the base word.

- Use `/s/` after a **voiceless non-sibilant sound** such as `/p/`, `/t/`, `/k/`, `/f/`:  
  > stops, works, laughs

- Use `/z/` after a **voiced non-sibilant sound** or a vowel sound:  
  > runs, plays, calls

- Use `/ɪz/` after **sibilant sounds** such as `/s/`, `/z/`, `/ʃ/`, `/ʒ/`, `/tʃ/`, `/dʒ/`:  
  > watches, uses, changes

This rule applies both to plural nouns and to third-person singular verbs:

> one report → two reports

> I work → she works

Do not add an extra syllable to every word ending in `-s`.

# 75. Giving Instructions

Clear instructions usually use imperatives.

> Open Settings.

> Select Network.

> Click Save.

For safety/caution:

> Make sure you back up the database first.

> Do not restart the server until the deployment is complete.

For optional steps:

> You can enable logging if you need additional details.

Good instructions are:

- ordered
- specific
- concise
- testable


## Include Preconditions and Expected Results

For technical instructions, state what must be true before the steps begin.

> **Prerequisite:** You must have administrator access.

After important steps, tell the reader what success looks like.

> **Expected result:** The status changes from `Pending` to `Approved`.

If a step is dangerous or irreversible, place the warning before the action:

> **Warning:** Back up the database before running the migration.

Instructions are strongest when another person can follow them without guessing what the writer intended.

# 84. Argument Structure

A persuasive argument usually contains:

1. Claim
2. Reason
3. Evidence/example
4. Counterargument
5. Response
6. Conclusion

> Companies should automate repetitive invoice validation because manual checking consumes significant employee time. A summary should contain the main ideas, not every detail.

> The production deployment failed because the certificate installed on the server had expired. The infrastructure team renewed the certificate, restarted the application pool, and confirmed that the application became available.

> Production was unavailable due to an expired certificate. The certificate was renewed and service was restored.

Ask:

<a id="chapter-86"></a>
# 86. Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing means expressing the same idea in different words without changing the meaning.

Original:

> The application failed because the database was unavailable.

Paraphrase:

> A database outage caused the application to fail.

Bad paraphrasing changes meaning or simply replaces every word with a synonym.

Good paraphrasing often changes:

- vocabulary
- sentence structure
- grammatical form

while preserving the original idea.

<a id="chapter-89"></a>
# 89. Register: Choosing the Right English

**Register** means the style of language suitable for a situation.

## Casual

> Hey, can you check this?

## Workplace neutral

> Hi, could you please check this?

## Formal

> Could you please review the attached document and share your feedback?

## Technical

> Please validate whether the API returns HTTP 200 for a successful request.

Good communication is not about always using advanced vocabulary. It is about choosing the right language for the audience.

<a id="chapter-90"></a>
# 90. Avoiding Unnecessarily Difficult English

Simple:

> We need to fix this before deployment.

Unnecessarily complex:

> It is incumbent upon us to remediate the aforementioned issue prior to the commencement of deployment activities.

Advanced English means **precision**, not maximum complexity.

Prefer:

> use

over:

> utilize

unless `utilize` has a specific reason.

Prefer:

> help

over:

> facilitate assistance

when the simpler form communicates the same idea.

<a id="chapter-91"></a>
# 91. Strong Verb Alternatives

Weak:

> make an improvement

Stronger:

> improve

Weak:

> make a decision

`make a decision` is completely natural, but when concise writing is needed:

> decide

Weak:

> conduct an investigation

Concise:

> investigate

Weak:

> provide an explanation

Concise:

> explain

Use noun-heavy forms when the style requires formality, but use strong verbs for clear writing.


## Concision Is Not Always Better

A strong verb is useful when it makes the sentence shorter without changing tone or meaning.

> We investigated the issue.

is usually clearer than:

> We conducted an investigation into the issue.

However, noun phrases can be useful when the action itself is a formal process:

> The audit committee conducted an investigation.

Choose the form that fits the context rather than replacing every noun phrase mechanically.

<a id="chapter-92"></a>
# 92. Hedging and Diplomatic Language

Hedging makes claims less absolute.

Strong:

> This will fail.

Diplomatic:

> This may fail under high load.

Strong:

> Your design is wrong.

Diplomatic:

> This design may create a scalability issue.

Useful hedges:

- may
- might
- could
- appears to
- seems to
- likely
- probably
- in some cases
- to some extent

Hedging is especially important when evidence is incomplete.

# 93. Precision: Numbers, Dates, and Times

Ambiguous:

> Finish it soon.

Precise:

> Please complete it by 3 PM on Friday.

Ambiguous:

> The server was down for some time.

Precise:

> The server was unavailable from 10:15 AM to 10:42 AM.

When communicating internationally, write dates clearly:

> 12 August 2026

> 12/08/26

<a id="chapter-95"></a>
# 95. Numbers in Spoken English

Practice:

- thirteen vs thirty
- fourteen vs forty
- fifteen vs fifty
- sixteen vs sixty

To avoid confusion:

> Did you say fifteen—one-five—or fifty—five-zero?

For decimals:

> 3.14 → three point one four

Percent:

> 25% → twenty-five percent

Large numbers:

> 1,500 → one thousand five hundred

> 1,000,000 → one million

Indian numbering terms such as `lakh` and `crore` are common in Indian English, but when communicating globally, consider converting to internationally familiar units such as thousands and millions when necessary.

<a id="chapter-96"></a>
# 96. Dates in Spoken English

Examples:

> 12 August

can be spoken:

> the twelfth of August

or:

> August twelfth

Years:

> 2026

often:

> twenty twenty-six

Always use the style that is clear to your audience.


## Avoid Date Ambiguity

Numeric dates can be interpreted differently across countries.

> 03/04/2026

could mean 3 April or March 4.

For international communication, write the month as a word:

> 3 April 2026

or:

> April 3, 2026

When speaking, ordinal numbers are common:

> 3 April → the third of April

> April 3 → April third

For deadlines, include the year when confusion is possible.

<a id="chapter-97"></a>
# 97. Abbreviations and Acronyms

An abbreviation shortens a word or phrase.

Examples:

> approx. = approximately

> dept. = department

An acronym/initialism uses initials:

> API

> URL

> HR

> ERP

When the audience may not know it, define it the first time:

> Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP)

Then use:

> ERP

Do not fill writing with unexplained abbreviations.


## Acronym vs Initialism

An **acronym** is commonly pronounced as a word, for example `NASA`.

An **initialism** is pronounced letter by letter, for example `API` or `HR`.

In everyday usage, people often use the word `acronym` loosely for both types.

### First-use rule

Write:

> Service Level Agreement (SLA)

Then later:

> The SLA requires a four-hour response.

Do not define very common abbreviations unnecessarily when the audience clearly knows them, but never assume a specialized internal acronym is globally understood.

# 98. English for Documentation

Good technical documentation answers:

1. What is this?
2. Why does it exist?
3. What does the reader need?
4. How do they use it?
5. What can go wrong?
6. How do they verify success?

Example structure:

```markdown
# Feature Name

## Purpose

## Prerequisites

## Configuration

## Usage

## Examples

## Error Handling

## Troubleshooting

## Security Notes

## FAQ
```

Technical writing should favor clarity, exact terms, and reproducible steps.

# 99. Writing Bug Reports

A useful bug report contains:

## Title

> Invoice upload fails for PDF files larger than 10 MB

## Environment

> UAT

## Steps to Reproduce

1. Open Invoice Upload.
2. Select a 12 MB PDF.
3. Click Upload.

## Expected

> The invoice should be uploaded successfully or the user should receive a documented size-limit message.

## Actual

> The page displays a generic 500 error.

## Evidence

- timestamp
- request ID
- screenshot
- log entry

Clear English directly improves engineering quality.

<a id="chapter-100"></a>
# 100. Writing Requirements

Avoid vague requirements.

Vague:

> System should be fast.

Better:

> For 95% of requests under normal operating load, the API should respond within two seconds.

Vague:

> User can upload file.

Better:

> The user can upload PDF, PNG, and JPEG files up to 15 MB.

Good requirements are:

- specific
- measurable
- testable
- unambiguous


## State Conditions and Acceptance Criteria

A requirement should explain not only what the system does but also important limits and conditions.

Example:

> The system shall allow an authenticated user to upload one PDF, PNG, or JPEG file up to 15 MB.

Useful acceptance criteria might state:

- accepted file types
- maximum size
- permission required
- expected success response
- expected validation message for unsupported files

Avoid subjective words such as `fast`, `easy`, or `user-friendly` unless they are converted into measurable criteria.

<a id="chapter-101"></a>
# 101. Writing Status Updates

A strong status update can use:

> Completed / In Progress / Blocked / Next

Example:

```text
Completed:
- API validation implemented.
- Unit tests added.

In Progress:
- UAT testing.

Blocked:
- Production credentials pending.

Next:
- Complete UAT.
- Prepare deployment package.
```

This structure is easier to scan than a long paragraph.


## Separate Facts From Forecasts

Example:

```text
Status:
- Development complete.
- 42 of 45 test cases passed.

Blocker:
- Production credentials pending.

Forecast:
- UAT is targeted for completion by 4:00 PM Friday.

Next:
- Retest three failed cases.
- Prepare deployment package after sign-off.
```

A forecast is not the same as a confirmed commitment. Use words such as `target`, `expected`, or `current ETA` when the time may still change.

# 102. Root-Cause Explanation Template

Use:

> Symptom → Scope → Cause → Fix → Prevention

Example:

> Users were unable to log in between 10:15 and 10:42 AM. The issue affected the production environment only. Investigation showed that the authentication certificate had expired. The infrastructure team renewed the certificate and restarted the affected service. We are adding certificate-expiry monitoring to reduce the risk of recurrence.

<a id="chapter-103"></a>
# 103. Giving Feedback

Good feedback focuses on behavior/work, not personal attack.

Weak:

> Your code is bad.

Better:

> This function is handling validation, database access, and response formatting in one place. Splitting those responsibilities may make it easier to test and maintain.

Useful pattern:

> Observation + Impact + Suggestion

> I noticed that the method has several nested conditions. This makes the flow difficult to follow. Could we extract some of the conditions into named functions?

<a id="chapter-104"></a>
# 104. Receiving Feedback

Useful responses:

> Thanks, that makes sense.

> I see what you mean.

> Could you give me an example?

> I'll update that.

> I had a different assumption. Let me explain what I was thinking.

You do not need to agree immediately with every suggestion. You can ask for evidence or clarify trade-offs professionally.


## A Useful Response Pattern

When receiving feedback:

> **Acknowledge → clarify if needed → decide action**

Example:

> Thanks, I understand the concern about the long introduction. Do you mean the first two paragraphs specifically? I'll shorten them and move the technical background to the appendix.

If you disagree, explain the trade-off rather than becoming defensive:

> I see the benefit of shortening it. My concern is that the audit team still needs the context. Could we keep a brief summary and move the detail to a separate section?

# 106. English for Conflict Resolution

Avoid personal statements.

Instead of:

> You never send the data on time.

Say:

> We've received the data after the agreed deadline several times. Could we identify what is causing the delay and agree on a reliable handoff process?

Useful language:

> I understand your concern.

> Let's focus on the specific issue.

> What outcome would work for both teams?

> Can we agree on the next step?

> Let's separate the immediate fix from the long-term solution.

<a id="chapter-107"></a>
# 107. English for Brainstorming

Encourage ideas:

> What other options do we have?

> What if we...

> Could we approach this differently?

Build on ideas:

> Building on Priya's point...

Challenge constructively:

> What risk would this create?

> How would this scale?

> What happens if the external service is unavailable?


## Separate Idea Generation From Evaluation

During early brainstorming, encourage possibilities:

> What else could work?

> What assumption are we making?

> Could we solve this without changing the database?

After ideas are collected, evaluate them:

> Which option has the lowest implementation risk?

> What would this cost?

> How would we test it?

This prevents every new idea from being rejected before the group has explored the problem.

<a id="chapter-108"></a>
# 108. English for Decision-Making

Useful structure:

> Options → Criteria → Trade-offs → Recommendation → Decision

Example:

> We have two options: process the file synchronously or move it to a background queue. The main criteria are response time, complexity, and reliability. A queue adds infrastructure complexity, but it handles long-running OCR jobs more reliably. I recommend the queue-based approach for large files.

<a id="chapter-109"></a>
# 109. Speaking About Uncertainty

Avoid pretending to know.

Useful:

> I don't know yet.

> I'm not certain.

> My current understanding is...

> Based on the available information...

> I need to verify that before confirming.

> That's an assumption, not a confirmed fact.

This language improves professional credibility.


## Separate Confidence Levels

Useful wording:

- **Confirmed:**  
  > The deployment completed at 3:15 PM.

- **Likely:**  
  > The issue is likely related to the certificate.

- **Possible:**  
  > The issue may be related to the certificate.

- **Unknown:**  
  > We have not yet confirmed the cause.

This prevents an assumption from sounding like a fact. In technical, legal, financial, and operational communication, certainty should match the evidence.

<a id="chapter-112"></a>
# 112. On Time vs In Time

`on time` = at the scheduled time.

> The train arrived on time.

`in time` = early enough before it is too late.

> We reached the station in time to catch the train.


## The Difference in Practice

Use **on time** when comparing an event with its schedule:

> The meeting started on time at 10:00 AM.

Use **in time** when there was enough time before a limit or event:

> We submitted the form in time to meet the deadline.

You can also say:

> just in time

meaning only slightly before it would have been too late.

> The backup finished just in time for the maintenance window.

<a id="chapter-113"></a>
# 113. Since vs For

`since` + starting point:

> since Monday

> since 2022

`for` + duration:

> for three days

> for five years

Example:

> I have lived here since 2020.

> I have lived here for six years.


## Use With Time Expressions

`since` points to the start:

> since Monday  
> since 2022  
> since I joined the company

`for` gives the length:

> for two hours  
> for three months  
> for a long time

With an action that started in the past and continues now, present perfect or present perfect continuous is common:

> I have lived here for six years.

> I have been working on this issue since 9:00 AM.

Do not use `since` with a duration such as `since five years`.

---

<a id="chapter-114"></a>
# 114. During vs For

`during` + event/period noun:

> during the meeting

`for` + duration:

> for two hours

Incorrect:

> during two hours

Correct:

> for two hours


## Grammar Pattern

`during` is followed by a noun phrase representing the event or period:

> during the meeting

> during the night

> during testing

`for` is followed by a duration:

> for 20 minutes

> for three weeks

Compare:

> The server failed **during the deployment**.

This tells us **when** it happened.

> The server was unavailable **for 20 minutes**.

This tells us **how long** it lasted.

<a id="chapter-115"></a>
# 115. By vs Until

`by` = deadline.

> Send it by Friday.

`until` = continuation.

> I will be available until Friday.


## Deadline vs Continuation

`by` means **no later than** a time:

> Please send the report by Friday.

Sending it Wednesday, Thursday, or Friday meets the deadline.

`until` describes a state or activity continuing up to a time:

> I will be available until Friday.

### Common mistake

Incorrect for a deadline:

> Please submit the form until Friday.

Better:

> Please submit the form by Friday.

Use `until` when the action itself continues:

> The support desk will remain open until 8:00 PM.

<a id="chapter-116"></a>
# 116. Say vs Tell

`say` normally does not need a person immediately after it:

> She said that she was busy.

`tell` normally takes a person:

> She told me that she was busy.

Correct:

> What did he say?

> What did he tell you?


## Common Patterns

Use `say` for the words or message:

> She said, "The test passed."

> She said that the test had passed.

Use `tell` when naming the listener:

> She told me that the test had passed.

Common patterns:

> say something

> say something to someone

> tell someone something

Incorrect:

> She told that the test passed.

Correct:

> She said that the test passed.

or:

> She told me that the test passed.

<a id="chapter-117"></a>
# 117. Speak vs Talk

Both can mean conversation.

> Can I speak to you?

> Can I talk to you?

`speak` can sound slightly more formal in some contexts and is used for languages:

> She speaks English.

Not:

> She talks English.


## Common Patterns

`speak` is common for languages, formal communication, and the ability to produce speech:

> She speaks Marathi and English.

> May I speak to the manager?

`talk` often emphasizes conversation:

> We talked about the deployment plan.

Both are possible in many situations:

> Can I speak to you for a minute?

> Can I talk to you for a minute?

Learn the prepositions too:

> speak **to/with** someone

> talk **to/with** someone

> talk **about** something

# 118. Make vs Do

- make an effort
- make a change

Common `do` collocations:

> make progress

<a id="chapter-120"></a>
# 120. Have: Common Uses

Possession:

> I have a car.

Experience:

> We had a good time.

Food:

> I had lunch.

Obligation:

> I have to leave.

Perfect tense:

> I have completed the task.

Causative structure:

> I had my laptop repaired.

Meaning: someone repaired it for me.


## `Have` Is Used in Several Different Structures

### Possession

> I have a laptop.

### Experience/activity

> We had a meeting.

> I had lunch.

### Obligation

> I have to leave by 5:00 PM.

### Perfect auxiliary

> I have completed the report.

Here `have` helps form the present perfect; it is not expressing possession.

### Causative

> I had my laptop repaired.

This means another person performed the repair for me.

Because `have` has many functions, identify the sentence pattern before deciding what it means.

<a id="chapter-122"></a>
# 122. Would Rather / Prefer

> I would rather stay home.

> I would rather use PostgreSQL than MySQL for this scenario.

`prefer`:

> I prefer tea to coffee.

> I prefer working from home.


## Grammar Patterns

`would rather` takes the base verb:

> I would rather wait.

Comparison:

> I would rather wait than deploy an untested build.

`prefer` can take a noun or `-ing` form:

> I prefer tea to coffee.

> I prefer working from home to commuting every day.

`would prefer` commonly takes `to + verb`:

> I would prefer to discuss this tomorrow.

Avoid:

> I would rather to wait.

Use:

> I would rather wait.

<a id="chapter-123"></a>
# 123. Too / Enough

`too` = more than desired:

> The file is too large.

`enough` = sufficient:

> The server has enough memory.

Adjective + enough:

> fast enough

Noun:

> enough time


## Position Matters

### `too`

> too + adjective/adverb

> The file is too large.

`too` usually means more than is acceptable or useful.

### `enough`

With an adjective/adverb:

> adjective/adverb + enough

> The connection is fast enough.

With a noun:

> enough + noun

> We have enough time.

Compare:

> The server is powerful enough to handle the load.

> The server is too slow to handle the load.

`very slow` describes degree; `too slow` means the slowness causes a problem.

<a id="chapter-124"></a>
# 124. So / Such

`so + adjective/adverb`

> The application is so slow.

`such + noun phrase`

> It is such a slow application.


## Main Patterns

Use:

> so + adjective/adverb

> The report is so detailed.

> She explained it so clearly.

Use:

> such + noun phrase

> It is such a detailed report.

> They are such useful examples.

With a singular countable noun, `such` usually includes `a/an`:

> such an important decision

With plural or uncountable nouns:

> such useful tools

> such good advice

Also common:

> so many requests

> so much work

<a id="chapter-125"></a>
# 125. Either / Neither Agreement

> Either option is acceptable.

> Neither option is acceptable.

With `either...or` / `neither...nor`, agreement can depend on the closest subject in many practical constructions:

> Either the manager or the developers are attending.

When a sentence feels awkward, rewrite it for clarity.


## Agreement With `Either...or` and `Neither...nor`

When the paired subjects differ in number, many style guides prefer the verb to agree with the nearer subject:

> Either the manager or the developers **are** attending.

> Either the developers or the manager **is** attending.

Because these sentences can sound awkward, rewriting is often clearer:

> Either the manager is attending, or the developers are.

With a single `either` or `neither`, the verb is normally singular in formal standard English:

> Neither option **is** suitable.

<a id="chapter-126"></a>
# 126. Tag Questions

Tag questions seek confirmation.

> You're coming, aren't you?

> She isn't available, is she?

> They finished, didn't they?

Intonation can change whether the speaker truly asks or merely seeks agreement.


## How Tag Questions Are Formed

A positive statement usually takes a negative tag:

> You're ready, aren't you?

A negative statement usually takes a positive tag:

> You aren't ready, are you?

The auxiliary verb normally matches the statement:

> She has finished, hasn't she?

> They can join, can't they?

With ordinary present/past verbs, use `do/does/did`:

> He works here, doesn't he?

> They left early, didn't they?

A rising intonation often sounds like a real question. Falling intonation often sounds more like confirmation.

<a id="chapter-127"></a>
# 127. Indirect Questions

Direct:

> Where is the server?

Polite indirect:

> Could you tell me where the server is?

Notice the word order:

Correct:

> Could you tell me where the server is?

Incorrect:

> Could you tell me where is the server?

Another:

> Do you know when the meeting starts?


## Indirect Questions Keep Statement Word Order

After the opening phrase, use:

> subject + verb

Direct:

> Why did the service fail?

Indirect:

> Could you explain why the service failed?

Not:

> Could you explain why did the service fail?

Common openings:

> Could you tell me...?

> Do you know...?

> Would you mind explaining...?

> I'm not sure...

Indirect questions are useful when you want a softer or more formal tone.

<a id="chapter-128"></a>
# 128. Question Softening

Direct:

> Why did you change this?

Softer:

> Could you help me understand why this was changed?

Direct:

> When will you finish?

Softer:

> Do you have an estimated completion date?

Softening is useful when maintaining a professional relationship matters.


## Softening Without Becoming Vague

Useful structures include:

> Could you help me understand...?

> Would you mind clarifying...?

> Do you happen to know...?

> When you have a moment, could you...?

The required action should still be clear.

Too vague:

> If possible, maybe perhaps you could have a look sometime.

Clear and polite:

> Could you please review the draft by Thursday afternoon?

Softening should improve tone, not remove the deadline or request.

<a id="chapter-129"></a>
# 129. Negative Questions

> Didn't you receive the email?

This often suggests the speaker expected that you received it.

Be careful: negative questions may sound surprised or challenging depending on tone.

Neutral alternative:

> Did you receive the email?


## Why Negative Questions Can Be Tricky

A negative question often carries an expectation:

> Didn't you receive the email?

The speaker may believe the email was sent and expect the answer to be yes.

For neutral information, prefer:

> Did you receive the email?

Answers to negative questions can also create cross-language confusion. When the fact matters, answer with a full statement:

> Yes, I received it.

> No, I did not receive it.

This is clearer than replying only `yes` or `no`.

---

<a id="chapter-130"></a>
# 130. Double Negatives

Standard English generally avoids double negatives when the intended meaning is negative.

Nonstandard:

> I don't know nothing.

Standard:

> I don't know anything.

or:

> I know nothing.


## Standard English vs Dialect

In standard formal English, two negative forms normally should not be used to express one negative meaning.

Standard:

> I don't know anything.

> I know nothing.

In some English dialects, multiple negatives are a regular grammatical feature:

> I don't know nothing.

That dialect form should not be treated as random language, but learners who need standard academic or professional English should use the standard patterns above.

---

<a id="chapter-131"></a>
# 131. Advanced Conditional Alternatives

Without `if`:

> Had I known, I would have acted differently.

Equivalent:

> If I had known, I would have acted differently.

> Were I responsible for the project, I would prioritize testing.

Equivalent:

> If I were responsible...

These are formal structures.


## Inverted Conditionals

Formal English can omit `if` and invert the auxiliary.

Third conditional:

> Had I known, I would have called.

Equivalent:

> If I had known, I would have called.

Formal hypothetical:

> Were the system unavailable, traffic would be redirected.

Equivalent:

> If the system were unavailable...

Formal future possibility:

> Should you need assistance, please contact support.

Equivalent:

> If you should need assistance...

These structures are useful in formal writing but unnecessary in ordinary conversation.

---

<a id="chapter-132"></a>
# 132. Wish and If Only

Present regret:

> I wish I had more time.

Past regret:

> I wish I had studied harder.

Complaint:

> I wish the server wouldn't restart automatically.

`If only` can express stronger emotion:

> If only I had backed up the file.


## Grammar Behind `Wish`

For an unreal present situation, English often uses a past form:

> I wish I had more time.

Meaning: I do not have enough time now.

For past regret, use past perfect:

> I wish I had backed up the file.

Meaning: I did not back it up.

For a desired change in behavior or situation, `would` can be used:

> I wish the service would stop restarting.

For ability:

> I wish I could speak more confidently.

`If only` uses similar patterns but often sounds more emotional or emphatic.

---

<a id="chapter-133"></a>
# 133. Causative Have/Get

When another person performs a service for you:

> I had my laptop repaired.

> I got my phone screen replaced.

You did not necessarily repair it yourself.


## Two Common Causative Patterns

### Have/get something done

> have/get + object + past participle

> I had the laptop repaired.

> I got the screen replaced.

The service was performed by someone else.

### Arrange for a person to do something

> have + person + base verb

> I had the technician check the cable.

> get + person + to-infinitive

> I got the technician to check the cable.

`get` is often more informal than `have`.

---

<a id="chapter-134"></a>
# 134. Modal Perfects

`modal + have + past participle`

## Should have

Past advice/regret:

> I should have checked the logs earlier.

## Could have

Past possibility:

> The deployment could have failed.

## Would have

Hypothetical result:

> I would have joined, but I was sick.

## Must have

Strong inference:

> The service must have restarted overnight.

## Might have

Possibility:

> The user might have entered the wrong password.

---

<a id="chapter-135"></a>
# 135. Advanced Passive Structures

> It is believed that the issue began yesterday.

> The issue is believed to have begun yesterday.

> The company is expected to announce the result tomorrow.

These structures are common in news and formal reports.


## Reporting Passives

Formal English often reports beliefs, expectations, or claims without making one person the grammatical subject.

> It is believed that the outage started at 2:00 AM.

Alternative:

> The outage is believed to have started at 2:00 AM.

Present/future expectation:

> The project is expected to finish in September.

Use these structures when the source is general or when the report itself is more important than the individual speaker. Do not use them to make an unsupported claim sound authoritative.

---

<a id="chapter-136"></a>
# 136. Punctuation for Advanced Writing

## Dash

Can add emphasis or interruption:

> The final blocker—the missing certificate—was resolved at noon.

## Parentheses

Add secondary information:

> The service was restarted (after approval from infrastructure).

## Quotation marks

Use for direct quotations or names/terms in certain style systems.

Avoid excessive punctuation. If a sentence needs too many punctuation marks, consider splitting it.

---

<a id="chapter-137"></a>
# 137. Paragraph Coherence

A paragraph should normally focus on one central idea.

Weak paragraph:

> The API failed. Our office is in Mumbai. We use Angular. The timeout was 30 seconds.

The ideas do not connect.

Coherent:

> The API failed because the OCR request exceeded the 30-second timeout. This occurred mainly for large PDF files. Increasing the timeout may reduce immediate failures, but moving long-running processing to a background job would be more scalable.

Each sentence supports the same topic.

---

<a id="chapter-138"></a>
# 138. Cohesion

Cohesion is how sentences connect to each other.

Tools:

- pronouns
- repeated key terms
- synonyms
- connectors
- logical sequence

Example:

> The team introduced a new validation layer. **This change** reduced invalid database writes. **As a result**, production errors decreased.


## Cohesion vs Coherence

**Cohesion** is the visible linguistic connection between sentences: pronouns, repeated terms, substitution, and connectors.

**Coherence** is the logical sense of the whole message.

A paragraph can be cohesive but still incoherent if the ideas do not follow logically.

Good paragraph:

> The API began timing out after the release. **This problem** affected large invoices. **As a result**, the team rolled back the change. **After the rollback**, response times returned to normal.

The repeated references and connectors create cohesion, while the cause-and-result order creates coherence.

<a id="chapter-139"></a>
# 139. Editing Checklist

Before sending important English writing, check:

## Meaning

- Is my main point obvious?
- Did I include the required action?
- Did I include dates or owners where necessary?

## Grammar

- Subject-verb agreement?
- Correct tense?
- Articles?
- Prepositions?

## Style

- Too many long sentences?
- Repeated words?
- Unnecessary formal language?
- Aggressive wording?

## Mechanics

- Spelling?
- Capitalization?
- Punctuation?

## Final question

> Could the reader misunderstand what I want?

<a id="chapter-140"></a>
# 140. Proofreading Method

Do not proofread everything at once.

Use passes.

Pass 1:

> Meaning and structure

Pass 2:

> Grammar

Pass 3:

> Spelling and punctuation

Pass 4:

> Conciseness

Pass 5:

> Read aloud

Reading aloud often reveals awkward English that looks fine on screen.


## Proofreading vs Editing

**Editing** improves meaning, organization, sentence structure, and wording.

**Proofreading** is the final check for smaller errors such as spelling, punctuation, missing words, and formatting.

A useful order is:

1. Fix the argument or message.
2. Improve paragraph and sentence structure.
3. Correct grammar and vocabulary.
4. Proofread spelling, punctuation, numbers, names, and formatting.

Do not spend ten minutes polishing a sentence that you may later delete because the paragraph itself is unnecessary.

<a id="chapter-141"></a>
# 141. Building an Active Vocabulary

You have two vocabularies:

## Passive Vocabulary

Words you understand when reading/listening.

## Active Vocabulary

Words you can use while speaking/writing.

The goal is to move important words from passive to active.

For every useful new word:

1. Say it.
2. Write it.
3. Create three sentences.
4. Use it in a conversation.
5. Review it later.

<a id="chapter-142"></a>
# 142. Vocabulary by Frequency

Do not begin by memorizing rare words.

Priority:

1. High-frequency everyday words
2. High-frequency work vocabulary
3. Vocabulary for your interests
4. Advanced and specialized vocabulary

Knowing the word `mitigate` is useful at work:

> mitigate risk

But knowing basic verbs such as `get`, `take`, `make`, `keep`, and `set` is far more important for everyday fluency.

<a id="chapter-143"></a>
# 143. Learning Chunks

A **chunk** is a group of words stored as one unit.

Examples:

> as far as I know

> at the end of the day

> could you please

> I'm not sure whether

> on the other hand

Native-like fluency depends heavily on chunks.

Instead of constructing every sentence word-by-word, your brain retrieves prebuilt patterns.


## Build Chunks Around Your Real Needs

Do not collect random expressions only because they sound advanced.

For meetings:

> Just to clarify...

> My understanding is that...

> The next step is...

For incidents:

> We are currently investigating...

> The current workaround is...

> The root cause has not yet been confirmed.

Practice replacing one part of the chunk while keeping the structure. This makes the phrase flexible rather than memorized as a fixed script.

<a id="chapter-145"></a>
# 145. Shadowing Program

Use 30–60 seconds of audio.

Day 1:
- Listen 5 times.

Day 2:
- Read transcript.
- Mark difficult phrases.

Day 3:
- Repeat sentence-by-sentence.

Day 4:
- Shadow audio in real time.

Day 5:
- Record yourself.

Day 6:
- Compare pronunciation and rhythm.

Day 7:
- Speak the content without the transcript.

This trains:

- listening
- pronunciation
- rhythm
- vocabulary
- grammar patterns
- speaking speed

<a id="chapter-146"></a>
# 146. Dictation Practice

Dictation is excellent for listening accuracy.

1. Play one short sentence.
2. Write exactly what you hear.
3. Compare with transcript.
4. Mark missed words.
5. Listen again.

Common things learners miss:

- articles
- prepositions
- contracted auxiliaries
- plural endings
- past endings
- reduced function words


## Use Short Audio and Repeat It

For beginners, 5–15 seconds of audio is enough for one dictation cycle.

After comparing with the transcript, classify errors:

- word completely unknown
- word known but not recognized in speech
- grammar ending missed
- reduced function word missed
- spelling error

The category tells you what to practice next. Repeating the same clip after correction is more useful than immediately moving to new audio.

---

<a id="chapter-147"></a>
# 147. Reading-Aloud Practice

Reading aloud helps:

- pronunciation
- rhythm
- confidence
- grammar awareness

But it does not replace spontaneous speaking.

Use both:

> Reading aloud → controlled production

> Free speaking → spontaneous production


## Use Reading Aloud Correctly

Reading aloud is useful for:

- noticing difficult sound combinations
- practicing sentence stress
- improving pausing
- hearing awkward written sentences

It is less useful for practicing spontaneous word retrieval because the words are already provided.

A balanced routine is:

1. Read a short paragraph aloud.
2. Close the text.
3. Summarize the same idea in your own words.
4. Listen to both attempts.

This combines controlled pronunciation practice with spontaneous speaking.

---

<a id="chapter-148"></a>
# 148. Free-Writing Practice

Set a timer for 10 minutes.

Write continuously without stopping to fix every error.

Topic:

> What happened at work today?

After writing:

1. Correct grammar.
2. Replace repeated words.
3. improve two sentences.
4. Save recurring mistakes.

This separates fluency practice from accuracy practice.


## Separate Drafting From Editing

During the timed part, keep writing even if a sentence is imperfect.

After the timer:

1. Underline unclear sentences.
2. Correct repeated grammar errors.
3. Replace one or two vague words.
4. Combine or split sentences where needed.
5. Rewrite one paragraph more clearly.

Do not try to perfect every sentence during the first draft. The exercise is designed to improve idea flow first and accuracy second.

---

<a id="chapter-149"></a>
# 149. The 4-3-2 Speaking Drill

Choose a topic.

Speak for 4 minutes.

Repeat the same topic in 3 minutes.

Repeat again in 2 minutes.

Because the content is already familiar, your delivery usually becomes faster, clearer, and more automatic.


## Why the 4-3-2 Drill Works

Use the **same topic and main ideas** in all three rounds.

Round 1 — 4 minutes: explain fully.

Round 2 — 3 minutes: repeat with less hesitation.

Round 3 — 2 minutes: keep the most important points and speak more efficiently.

Do not try to memorize an exact script. The purpose is faster retrieval and smoother organization.

After the drill, note:

- repeated pauses
- words you searched for
- grammar errors that affected meaning
- useful phrases that became easier

---

<a id="chapter-150"></a>
# 150. Question-Answer Drill

Choose a topic and generate questions.

Topic: work

> What do you do?

> What technologies do you use?

> What is the most difficult part of your job?

> What project are you currently working on?

> What do you want to learn next?

Answer each aloud.

This is very effective for interview preparation.


## Vary the Difficulty

Begin with factual questions:

> What do you do?

Then add explanation:

> Why did you choose this field?

Then add opinion or problem-solving:

> What would you change about your current workflow?

For interview practice, answer once in 30 seconds and again in 90 seconds. This teaches you to control answer length instead of memorizing one fixed script.

<a id="chapter-152"></a>
# 152. How to Correct Yourself While Speaking

Do not panic after a mistake.

Natural repair:

> He go—sorry, he **went** to the office yesterday.

> We've completed the deployment—actually, the **UAT** deployment, not production.

Self-correction is normal and often shows good language awareness.


## Correct Only What Needs Repair

Natural speakers also restart and correct themselves.

Useful repair phrases:

> Sorry, I mean...

> Let me correct that...

> More precisely...

> What I meant was...

Example:

> The deployment is on Thursday—sorry, I mean **Friday**.

If the mistake does not affect meaning, you do not need to interrupt every sentence to repair it. During fluency practice, excessive self-correction can make speech harder to follow.

---

<a id="chapter-153"></a>
# 153. How to Avoid Freezing

When you need time:

> Let me think for a moment.

> There are a couple of things to consider.

> The first thing that comes to mind is...

Then begin with a simple sentence.

Do not search mentally for the "perfect" advanced sentence.


## Use a Holding Phrase, Then Start Simple

A holding phrase should buy a few seconds, not become a long delay.

> That's a useful question. The main point is...

> Let me think for a moment. I would approach it in two steps.

If you do not know a word, describe it:

> It's the tool we use to monitor server health.

This keeps the conversation moving while your vocabulary catches up.

<a id="chapter-154"></a>
# 154. Fluency vs Accuracy

You need both, but not always at the same moment.

## Accuracy Practice

Slow down.

Focus on:

- grammar
- exact vocabulary
- pronunciation

## Fluency Practice

Keep going.

Focus on:

- communicating
- reducing pauses
- organizing ideas

Trying to be perfectly accurate during every fluency exercise can make you afraid to speak.


## Practice Them Separately, Then Combine Them

Example weekly balance:

- **Accuracy session:** grammar exercises, pronunciation correction, careful writing.
- **Fluency session:** timed speaking, free writing, conversation.
- **Integrated session:** real email, meeting simulation, or presentation where both matter.

During real communication, correct errors that change meaning or professionalism first. Minor errors can be reviewed afterward.

<a id="chapter-155"></a>
# 155. English Learning Metrics

Track useful metrics instead of vague feelings.

Weekly:

- minutes listened
- minutes spoken
- pages read
- words written
- new phrases actively used
- repeated mistakes corrected

Monthly:

- 5-minute speaking recording
- one professional email sample
- one listening test
- one vocabulary review

Compare yourself with your previous performance, not only with fluent speakers.


## Track Quality, Not Only Quantity

Listening for 300 minutes is useful only if the material is understandable and you are learning from it.

Add quality measures:

- percentage of a short clip understood
- number of recurring errors reduced
- number of phrases used correctly without notes
- clarity of a monthly speaking recording
- ability to complete a real task in English

Metrics should show progress toward communication, not turn learning into a competition for large numbers.

<a id="chapter-158"></a>
# 158. English Learning Resource Selection Checklist

Choose resources that are:

- understandable but slightly challenging
- relevant to your goals
- available with transcripts when possible
- spoken clearly
- interesting enough to continue
- varied across accents and situations

Avoid changing learning resources every few days.

One good resource used consistently is better than twenty unused resources.


## Match the Resource to the Skill

For pronunciation, choose material with clear audio and replay controls.

For listening, transcripts are especially useful for checking missed words.

For reading, choose material where you understand most of the text without checking every sentence.

For professional English, prefer realistic emails, meetings, presentations, and documentation relevant to your work.

A resource can be excellent but still wrong for your current goal or level.

<a id="chapter-159"></a>
# 159. How to Use a Dictionary Properly

When checking a word, look at:

1. Meaning
2. Pronunciation
3. Part of speech
4. Example
5. Collocations
6. Grammar pattern
7. Formality/register
8. Other forms

For example, do not only learn:

> depend = rely

Also learn:

> depend **on**

> It depends on the situation.


## Read More Than the First Definition

A dictionary entry may show several meanings for one word.

For `issue`, you might find meanings related to:

- a problem
- a topic
- publishing/distributing something
- an edition of a publication

Use the example sentence and context to choose the correct sense.

Also check whether the dictionary labels a word as:

- formal
- informal
- technical
- old-fashioned
- regional

This helps you avoid using a correct word in an inappropriate situation.

<a id="chapter-165"></a>
# 165. The Ultimate Rule: Prefer Clear English

Compare:

> The aforementioned implementation methodology necessitates further optimization in relation to performance-related considerations.

With:

> The current implementation needs further performance optimization.

The second sentence is better.

It is measured by whether you can express the **right meaning**, to the **right person**, in the **right tone**, with the **least unnecessary friction**.
