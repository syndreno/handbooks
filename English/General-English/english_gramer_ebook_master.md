This handbook is designed for learners who want one place to study grammar, spoken English, vocabulary, sentence building, conversation, email writing, interview English, and practical everyday usage.

The source books sometimes repeat the same topic, use Hindi-first explanations, or contain examples that are old-fashioned, unnatural, or grammatically incorrect. In this master handbook:

- repeated topics are merged;
- the simplest explanation comes first;
- useful source examples are preserved when appropriate;
- incorrect or unnatural examples are corrected;
- difficult ideas receive easier examples;
- important gaps are filled with editor-added explanations;

# Part I - Foundations

## 1. What Is Grammar?

**Grammar is the system that tells us how words work together to make meaningful sentences.** You do not need to memorize every grammar term before speaking. You need to understand the most useful patterns and practise them until they become natural.

Compare:

- Incorrect: `She go office every day.`
- Correct: `She goes to the office every day.`

The correction involves several grammar ideas: subject-verb agreement (`she goes`), a preposition (`to`), and an article (`the`).

### Grammar is a tool, not the final goal

The goal of grammar is clear communication. In real conversation, a short natural sentence is usually better than a complicated sentence that you cannot use confidently.

---

## 2. The Basic English Sentence

The most important beginner pattern is:

> **Subject + Verb + Object/Complement**

### 2.1 Subject

The **subject** tells us who or what performs the action or is being described.

- **Riya** works.
- **The children** are playing.
- **My computer** is slow.

### 2.2 Verb

The **verb** shows an action, event, or state.

- Riya **works**.
- They **laughed**.
- I **am** tired.
- He **knows** the answer.

### 2.3 Object

The **object** receives the action.

- She reads **books**.
- I called **Rahul**.
- They bought **a laptop**.

Not every sentence needs an object:

- The baby slept.
- We laughed.
- The bus arrived.

### 2.4 Complement

A **complement** completes the meaning after linking verbs such as *be, seem, become, look,* and *feel*.

- She is **happy**.
- He became **a manager**.
- The soup tastes **good**.

### Useful sentence patterns

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Subject + Verb | Birds fly. |
| Subject + Verb + Object | I read books. |
| Subject + be + Complement | She is tired. |
| Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object | He gave me a gift. |
| Subject + Verb + Object + Complement | They elected her captain. |
| There + be + noun | There is a problem. |

---


### 2.5 Four basic sentence forms

The tense and spoken-English books repeatedly practise four sentence shapes. Learn these early because the same idea appears in almost every tense.

| Form | Meaning | Example with **be** | Example with an action verb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Affirmative | says something is true | She is ready. | She works here. |
| Negative | says something is not true | She is not ready. | She does not work here. |
| Interrogative | asks a question | Is she ready? | Does she work here? |
| Negative interrogative | asks a negative question | Isn't she ready? | Doesn't she work here? |

The basic rule is:

- With **be**, move the form of *be* before the subject to make a question.
- With a **modal**, move the modal before the subject: `Can you swim?`
- In the present simple and past simple, use **do/does/did** for most questions and negatives:
  - `Do you work here?`
  - `She doesn't work here.`
  - `Did they call you?`
- After **do, does,** or **did**, use the base form of the main verb:
  - Correct: `Does he go?`
  - Incorrect: `Does he goes?`

Negative questions can express surprise, expectation, or a request for confirmation:

> Aren't you coming with us?

> Didn't you receive my email?

Do not overuse negative questions when a neutral question is enough.

### 2.6 This, that, these, and those

The spoken-English material gives these words a separate lesson because they are used constantly in real conversation.

| Distance/idea | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| near the speaker / current idea | this | these |
| farther away / earlier idea | that | those |

Examples:

- `This is my phone.` - one thing near me.
- `These are my keys.` - more than one thing near me.
- `That is your chair.` - one thing farther away.
- `Those are our bags.` - more than one thing farther away.

They can be **pronouns** when they stand alone:

> This is useful.

They can be **determiners** before a noun:

> This book is useful.

Common mistakes:

- Incorrect: `This books are mine.`
- Correct: `These books are mine.`

- Incorrect: `These is my file.`
- Correct: `This is my file.`

`That` can also introduce a clause:

> I think **that** he is right.

That use is different from the demonstrative use above.


## 3. The Eight Parts of Speech

Words are commonly grouped according to the job they do in a sentence.

| Part of speech | Main job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | names a person, place, thing, or idea | teacher, Mumbai, table, freedom |
| Pronoun | replaces a noun | I, you, he, they, which |
| Adjective | describes a noun/pronoun | helpful, red, three |
| Verb | shows action/state | run, think, be |
| Adverb | modifies a verb/adjective/adverb | slowly, very, often |
| Preposition | shows relationship | in, on, at, with |
| Conjunction | joins words/clauses | and, but, because |
| Interjection | expresses sudden feeling | Oh!, Wow!, Ouch! |

A single word can sometimes belong to different categories depending on its job:

- `I have a **fast** car.` - *fast* is an adjective.
- `He runs **fast**.` - *fast* is an adverb.

# Part II - Parts of Speech in Depth

## 4. Nouns

A **noun** names a person, place, thing, group, quality, feeling, or idea.

### 4.1 Common noun types

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Proper noun | specific name | India, Monday, Priya |
| Common noun | general name | country, day, student |
| Collective noun | group treated as one unit | team, family, committee |
| Material noun | substance/material | gold, water, wood |
| Abstract noun | idea, quality, state | honesty, love, freedom |
| Concrete noun | physically observable thing | chair, dog, apple |

### 4.2 Countable and uncountable nouns

**Countable nouns** can be counted:

- one book, two books
- a chair, several chairs

**Uncountable nouns** are normally not counted as separate units:

- water
- information
- advice
- furniture
- news
- luggage
- homework

Common mistakes:

- Incorrect: `an advice`
- Correct: `some advice` / `a piece of advice`

- Incorrect: `many informations`
- Correct: `a lot of information`

- Incorrect: `What a news!`
- Correct: `What great news!`

### 4.3 Singular and plural nouns

Common rules:

- book -> books
- bus -> buses
- city -> cities
- leaf -> leaves (many, but not all, *-f/-fe* nouns)
- child -> children
- man -> men
- mouse -> mice

Do not assume every noun ending in **-s** is plural. *News* is singular in standard English:

- `The news is surprising.`

### 4.4 Possessive nouns

Use **'s** with a singular noun:

- Riya's bag
- the teacher's desk

Use an apostrophe after a regular plural ending in **s**:

- the students' books

Irregular plurals take **'s**:

- children's clothes
- men's shoes

---

## 5. Pronouns

A **pronoun** replaces a noun so that we do not repeat the same name again and again.

> Rahul is my colleague. **He** works with **me**.

### 5.1 Personal pronouns

| Subject | Object | Possessive adjective | Possessive pronoun | Reflexive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | me | my | mine | myself |
| you | you | your | yours | yourself/yourselves |
| he | him | his | his | himself |
| she | her | her | hers | herself |
| it | it | its | - | itself |
| we | us | our | ours | ourselves |
| they | them | their | theirs | themselves |

Examples:

- **She** called **me**.
- This is **my** book. The book is **mine**.
- He taught **himself** Python.

### 5.2 Other pronoun types

**Demonstrative:** this, that, these, those  
`This is my seat.`

**Interrogative:** who, whom, whose, which, what  
`Who called you?`

**Relative:** who, whom, whose, which, that  
`The woman who called me is my manager.`

**Indefinite:** someone, anyone, everybody, nothing, few, many  
`Someone is waiting outside.`

**Distributive:** each, either, neither  
`Each has a different role.`

**Reciprocal:** each other, one another  
`They helped each other.`


### Emphatic (intensive) pronouns

The grammar source separates **reflexive** and **emphatic/intensive** uses of the same `-self/-selves` forms.

**Reflexive:** the subject and object refer to the same person or thing.

> I hurt **myself**.

**Emphatic/intensive:** the form adds emphasis but is not required to complete the sentence.

> I fixed the computer **myself**.

> The manager **herself** explained the policy.

A useful test: if you remove the `-self/-selves` word and the sentence is still grammatically complete, it is usually emphatic/intensive rather than reflexive.

### Source-style pronoun classification at a glance

The grammar ebook teaches ten traditional pronoun categories. Modern grammar books may group them slightly differently, but the categories are useful for learning.

| Type | Examples | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| personal | I, we, you, he, she, it, they | refers to people/things |
| possessive | mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs | shows possession without a following noun |
| reflexive | myself, yourself, herself, ourselves | refers back to the subject |
| emphatic/intensive | myself, yourself, themselves | adds emphasis |
| demonstrative | this, that, these, those | points to something |
| indefinite | someone, anyone, none, everybody | refers non-specifically |
| distributive | each, either, neither | considers members separately |
| reciprocal | each other, one another | shows a mutual relationship |
| relative | who, which, that, whose | introduces a relative clause |
| interrogative | who, what, which, whose | asks a question |


### 5.3 Pronoun agreement

The pronoun must clearly refer to the correct noun.

Unclear:

> When Raj spoke to Amit, **he** was angry.

Who was angry? Raj or Amit? Rewrite:

> Raj was angry when he spoke to Amit.

### Singular *they*

Modern English commonly uses **they/them/their** for a person whose gender is unknown or irrelevant:

> If a customer calls, ask **them** for **their** order number.

## 6. Adjectives

An **adjective** describes a noun or pronoun.

- a **helpful** person
- a **large** room
- **three** files
- this **old** laptop

### Common adjective categories

- **Quality:** beautiful, kind, difficult
- **Quantity:** some, much, little
- **Number:** one, two, first, several
- **Demonstrative:** this, that, these, those
- **Possessive:** my, your, his, her
- **Distributive:** each, every, either, neither
- **Interrogative:** which, what, whose
- **Proper:** Indian food, French language
- **Emphasizing (traditional label):** own, very - `my own room`, `the very same day`
- **Exclamatory (traditional label):** what - `What a beautiful day!`

Modern grammar may classify words such as *my, this, every,* and *what* as **determiners** rather than ordinary adjectives in these uses. The traditional labels are still useful because they appear in many school grammar books.

### 6.1 Adjective order

When several adjectives come before a noun, natural English often follows this rough order:

> opinion -> size -> age -> shape -> colour -> origin -> material -> purpose -> noun

Example:

> a beautiful small old brown Italian leather bag

Do not overload a noun with too many adjectives in normal speech.

### 6.2 Adjective or adverb?

- `She is a **careful** driver.` - adjective modifies *driver*.
- `She drives **carefully**.` - adverb modifies *drives*.

---

## 7. Verbs

A **verb** shows an action, event, possession, thought, feeling, or state.

### 7.1 Main verbs and helping verbs

**Main verb:** carries the main meaning.

- She **works**.
- They **eat** lunch.

**Helping/auxiliary verb:** helps the main verb express tense, question, negation, voice, or meaning.

- She **is working**.
- **Do** you work here?
- They **have finished**.
- You **should study**.

### 7.2 Transitive and intransitive verbs

A **transitive verb** takes an object:

> She opened **the door**.

An **intransitive verb** does not need an object:

> The baby slept.

Some verbs can be both:

- `The bell rang.`
- `She rang the bell.`

This matters for passive voice. Only verbs that can take an object normally form a useful passive.

### 7.3 Stative verbs

Some verbs usually describe states rather than actions, so they are not normally used in continuous forms when they have their ordinary state meaning:

- know
- believe
- understand
- want
- need
- own
- belong
- love

Natural:

> I love you.

Usually unnatural:

> I am loving you.

Some stative verbs can become dynamic when the meaning changes:

- `I think you are right.` = opinion
- `I am thinking about the problem.` = mental activity now

## 8. Adverbs

An **adverb** often tells us how, when, where, how often, or to what degree something happens.

### Common types

| Type | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Manner | How? | slowly, carefully |
| Time | When? | now, yesterday, soon |
| Place | Where? | here, outside, nearby |
| Frequency | How often? | always, often, never |
| Degree | How much? | very, quite, almost |
| Certainty | How sure? | probably, definitely |


The source grammar also uses several traditional labels that are worth recognizing:

| Traditional label | Typical words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| interrogative adverb | when, where, why, how | `Why are you late?` |
| affirmation/negation adverb | certainly, definitely, not, never | `She certainly knows.` |
| reason/result adverb | therefore, consequently | `It was late; therefore, we left.` |
| relative adverb | when, where, why | `I remember the day when we met.` |

In modern grammar, words such as **therefore** are often called *conjunctive adverbs*, and some classifications differ from textbook to textbook. Learn the function of the word in the sentence rather than worrying too much about the label.


### Position of frequency adverbs

Usually before the main verb:

- I **often go** there.
- She **never eats** meat.

But after *be*:

- He **is always** helpful.

### Good vs well

- `She is a **good** singer.`
- `She sings **well**.`

*Good* is normally an adjective; *well* is normally an adverb.

## 9. Prepositions

A **preposition** shows a relationship involving place, time, direction, method, reason, or connection.

### 9.1 In, on, at for time

| Preposition | Typical use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| at | exact time | at 7:00, at noon |
| on | day/date | on Monday, on 15 August |
| in | month/year/long period | in June, in 2026, in winter |

### 9.2 In, on, at for place

- **at** = a point: `at the station`
- **in** = inside an area/space: `in Mumbai`, `in the room`
- **on** = surface: `on the table`

### 9.3 Since and for

- **since** + starting point: `since Monday`, `since 2020`
- **for** + duration: `for two hours`, `for five years`

### 9.4 By and until

- **by** = no later than a deadline: `Finish it by Friday.`
- **until/till** = continue up to a time: `I will stay until Friday.`


### 9.5 Direction and position

The grammar and spoken-English books contain many position expressions. These are especially useful for directions, seating arrangements, travel, and describing objects.

| Expression | Simple meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| to | destination | We went **to** the station. |
| toward/towards | in the direction of | She walked **towards** the gate. |
| into | movement from outside to inside | He went **into** the room. |
| out of | movement from inside to outside | She came **out of** the office. |
| across | from one side to the other, usually on a surface/open area | We walked **across** the road. |
| through | from one side to another inside something | The train went **through** the tunnel. |
| along | following a line/path | We walked **along** the river. |
| past | beyond a point | Go **past** the bank. |
| via | by way of | We flew to London **via** Dubai. |
| beside / next to | at the side of | Sit **beside** me. |
| adjacent to | next to; more formal | The office is **adjacent to** the bank. |
| in front of | before something in space | The car is **in front of** the house. |
| behind | at the back of | He is standing **behind** me. |
| ahead of | farther forward | Our bus is **ahead of** theirs. |
| between | in the middle of two or clearly separate items | She sat **between** Ravi and Maya. |
| among | in a group of three or more | He was standing **among** his friends. |
| above | at a higher level, not necessarily directly over | A lamp hangs **above** the table. |
| over | above; often directly covering/crossing | The plane flew **over** the city. |
| below | at a lower level | The temperature fell **below** zero. |
| under | below; often directly beneath | The cat is **under** the table. |

#### Position-language corrections

Natural:

- `Shalini is sitting **to the left of** Ram.`
- `Rashmi is sitting **immediately to the right of** Divya.`
- `Mayank is sitting **behind** Sandeep.`
- `Ram is sitting **next to** Mayank.`

Avoid forms such as `left to Ram` or `immediate right to Divya` in standard international English.

#### Beside vs besides

- **beside** = next to: `Sit beside me.`
- **besides** = in addition to: `Besides English, she speaks Hindi.`

#### Before vs in front of

**Before** commonly refers to time or order:

> Finish this before Friday.

**In front of** normally refers to physical position:

> The taxi is in front of the hotel.


### 9.6 Common verb-preposition combinations

- depend **on**
- listen **to**
- wait **for**
- apply **for** a job
- apply **to** a company
- belong **to**
- agree **with** a person/opinion
- agree **on** a topic/plan
- suffer **from**
- focus **on**
- participate **in**

Common error:

- Incorrect: `discuss about the issue`
- Correct: `discuss the issue`

## 10. Conjunctions

A **conjunction** joins words, phrases, or clauses.

### 10.1 Coordinating conjunctions

**FANBOYS:** for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so

> I was tired, **but** I finished the work.

### 10.2 Subordinating conjunctions

These connect a dependent clause to a main clause:

- because
- although
- if
- when
- while
- before
- after
- unless
- since

> I stayed home **because** I was sick.

> **Although** it was raining, we went out.

### 10.3 Correlative conjunctions

These work in pairs:

- both ... and
- either ... or
- neither ... nor
- not only ... but also

> She is **not only** intelligent **but also** hardworking.

Keep the grammar parallel:

- Better: `He likes reading, swimming, and cycling.`
- Weaker: `He likes reading, to swim, and cycling.`

---

## 11. Interjections

An **interjection** expresses a sudden reaction or feeling.

- Wow! That is amazing.
- Oh! I forgot the key.
- Ouch! That hurt.
- Great! We finished early.

Use them carefully in formal writing because many are conversational.

# Part III - Articles, Determiners, and Agreement

## 12. Articles: A, An, and The

### 12.1 The basic rule

**A/an** means one non-specific member of a group and is used with a **singular countable noun**.

- a book
- a teacher
- an apple
- an idea

**The** points to something specific or already known and can be used with singular, plural, or uncountable nouns.

- the book on the table
- the students in my class
- the water in this bottle

### 12.2 A or an depends on sound

Use **a** before a consonant sound:

- a book
- a university (`university` begins with a /y/ sound)
- a one-time payment (`one` begins with a /w/ sound)
- a European country

Use **an** before a vowel sound:

- an apple
- an hour (silent *h*)
- an honest person
- an MBA graduate (pronounced *em-bee-ay*)

Source-style error corrected:

- Incorrect: `an hair`
- Correct: `a hair`

### 12.3 Common uses of *the*

Use *the* when:

- both speaker and listener know the thing: `Close the door.`
- it was mentioned earlier: `I saw a dog. The dog was friendly.`
- the noun is unique in context: `the sun`, `the CEO of this company`
- a superlative is used: `the best`, `the largest`
- an ordinal refers to a specific item: `the first chapter`
- a defining phrase makes the noun specific: `the man in the blue shirt`

### 12.4 Zero article

Often use no article with:

- plural nouns in a general sense: `Books are useful.`
- uncountable nouns in a general sense: `Water is essential.`
- languages: `She speaks English.`
- most countries/cities: `India`, `Mumbai`
- days/months: `Monday`, `July`
- meals in a general sense: `We had breakfast.`

But context can make a noun specific:

> The breakfast served at the hotel was excellent.

### 12.5 More important uses of *the*

Use **the** with many names that describe a group, collection, or geographical feature:

- rivers: `the Ganges`, `the Nile`
- seas and oceans: `the Arabian Sea`, `the Indian Ocean`
- mountain ranges: `the Himalayas`
- groups of islands: `the Andaman and Nicobar Islands`
- deserts: `the Sahara`
- many plural/descriptive country names: `the United States`, `the United Kingdom`, `the Netherlands`
- well-known institutions/buildings when the name includes a common noun: `the Supreme Court`, `the Taj Mahal`
- musical instruments in some general statements: `She plays the piano.`
- adjective groups referring to people as a class: `the rich`, `the elderly` (use with care; person-first wording may sometimes be preferable)

But normally use **no article** with single mountains, most lakes, cities, continents, and most countries:

- Mount Everest
- Lake Victoria
- Asia
- Japan

### 12.6 Titles, jobs, and positions

Use **a/an** when a job is one of many possible jobs:

- She is **an engineer**.
- He became **a manager**.

Use **the** when the position is unique or specifically identified:

- She is **the manager of this branch**.

After verbs such as **elect, appoint, make, name**, a title or position can appear without an article when it functions as a complement:

- They elected her **president**.
- He was appointed **chairperson**.

This explains why adding *the* mechanically after phrases such as `the rank of` or before every job title can be wrong.

### 12.7 Institutions and purpose

British-style English often omits **the** when certain institutions are used for their normal purpose:

- go to school
- be at university
- go to bed
- be in prison
- go to church

Use **the** when referring to the specific building/place rather than its normal institutional purpose:

- I went to **the school** to meet the principal.
- The workers repaired **the prison**.

Usage differs somewhat between British and American English, especially with **hospital** and **university**. Learn the variety you need, but understand the underlying distinction between **institutional purpose** and **specific place**.

### 12.8 Articles with illnesses, meals, transport, and abstract nouns

Often no article is used with:

- meals generally: `We had lunch at noon.`
- transport after *by*: `by bus`, `by train`, `by air`
- abstract ideas in a general sense: `Honesty matters.`
- many illnesses: `She has malaria.`

But a specific or countable form can require an article:

- `The lunch we ordered was late.`
- `I took the 8:30 train.`
- `He has a cold.`

Do not memorize "never use an article with X" unless the rule is truly absolute. Context often changes a general noun into a specific one.

### Corrected source example

- Incorrect: `The Sun Rise In The East.`
- Correct: `The sun rises in the east.`

## 13. Determiners

Determiners come before nouns and help identify or limit them.

Common groups:

- articles: a, an, the
- demonstratives: this, that, these, those
- possessives: my, your, his, her, our, their
- quantifiers: some, any, much, many, few, little, enough
- numbers: one, two, first, second
- distributives: each, every, either, neither

### Much vs many

- many + countable plural: `many books`
- much + uncountable: `much time`

In positive everyday English, *a lot of* is often more natural:

> We have a lot of work.

### Few vs a few

- **few** = almost none; negative idea
- **a few** = some; positive/neutral idea

> Few people understood. = almost nobody understood.  
> A few people understood. = some people understood.

### Little vs a little

Same distinction with uncountable nouns:

> I have little time. = almost no time.  
> I have a little time. = some time.

## 14. Subject-Verb Agreement

The verb must agree with the subject in number and person.

### Basic rule

- I/you/we/they **work**.
- he/she/it **works**.

With *be*:

- I **am**
- you/we/they **are**
- he/she/it **is**

### Common patterns

**Each/every/everyone/everybody/someone** normally takes a singular verb:

> Everyone is ready.

**Two subjects joined by and** usually take a plural verb:

> Riya and Sameer are here.

With **either...or / neither...nor**, the verb often agrees with the nearer subject in standard usage:

> Either the manager or the employees are attending.  
> Either the employees or the manager is attending.

Collective nouns vary by dialect and meaning. In American English, a group acting as one unit commonly takes a singular verb:

> The team is winning.

### Common mistakes

- Incorrect: `She go to work.`
- Correct: `She goes to work.`

- Incorrect: `He don't know.`
- Correct: `He doesn't know.`

- Incorrect: `Manish need a help.`
- Correct: `Manish needs help.`

# Part IV - Verb Forms and Tenses

## 15. The Main Verb Forms

Traditional learning books often label verb forms as V1, V2, V3, V4, and V5.

| Label | Meaning | Example with *write* |
|---|---|---|
| V1 | base form | write |
| V2 | past form | wrote |
| V3 | past participle | written |
| V4 | -ing form/present participle | writing |
| V5 | third-person singular present | writes |

Examples:

- I **write** every day. (V1)
- I **wrote** yesterday. (V2)
- I have **written** the email. (V3)
- I am **writing** now. (V4)
- She **writes** every day. (V5)

### Regular and irregular verbs

Regular verbs normally add **-ed**:

- work - worked - worked
- open - opened - opened

Irregular verbs change in different ways:

- go - went - gone
- eat - ate - eaten
- write - wrote - written
- put - put - put

A larger irregular-verb reference appears later in this handbook.

---


### 15.1 Regular verb spelling rules

The verb-form books mainly present V1/V2/V3 lists. These spelling rules help you form regular verbs correctly.

**Past and past participle (-ed)**

- work -> worked
- live -> lived
- study -> studied
- stop -> stopped

Rules:

1. If the verb already ends in **e**, add **-d**: `live -> lived`.
2. If it ends in consonant + **y**, change **y** to **i** and add **-ed**: `study -> studied`.
3. For many short stressed verbs ending consonant-vowel-consonant, double the final consonant: `stop -> stopped`, `plan -> planned`.
4. Do not double **w, x,** or **y**: `snowed`, `fixed`, `played`.

**-ing forms**

- work -> working
- make -> making
- run -> running
- lie -> lying

Common rules:

- usually add **-ing**;
- drop a final silent **e**: `make -> making`;
- double the final consonant in many short stressed consonant-vowel-consonant verbs: `run -> running`;
- `ie` usually becomes `y`: `lie -> lying`, `die -> dying`.

### 15.2 Useful irregular-verb patterns

Irregular verbs do not follow one spelling rule, but grouping them makes memorization easier.

**All three forms the same**

- cut - cut - cut
- put - put - put
- hit - hit - hit
- cost - cost - cost

**V2 and V3 the same**

- buy - bought - bought
- bring - brought - brought
- teach - taught - taught
- build - built - built
- feel - felt - felt
- keep - kept - kept

**All three forms different**

- write - wrote - written
- drive - drove - driven
- sing - sang - sung
- take - took - taken
- see - saw - seen
- go - went - gone

### 15.3 Lie and lay

These verbs are frequently confused.

| Meaning | V1 | V2 | V3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| rest/recline; no object | lie | lay | lain |
| put something down; takes an object | lay | laid | laid |
| say something untrue | lie | lied | lied |

Examples:

> I **lay** on the sofa yesterday.  
> I have **lain** here for an hour.

> Please **lay** the book on the table.  
> She **laid** it there yesterday.

> He **lied** about the result.


## 16. Understanding Tense and Aspect

English learning materials commonly teach **12 tense forms** by combining three time frames with four aspects.

| Time | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect continuous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Present | I work | I am working | I have worked | I have been working |
| Past | I worked | I was working | I had worked | I had been working |
| Future | I will work | I will be working | I will have worked | I will have been working |

A useful way to understand them:

- **Simple** - facts, habits, complete events.
- **Continuous** - activity in progress or temporary situation.
- **Perfect** - connects one time to an earlier event/result.
- **Perfect continuous** - emphasizes duration or ongoing activity across a time period.

Do not choose a tense only by translating Hindi markers. Ask: **What time is it? Is the action complete, ongoing, repeated, or connected to another time?**


### 16.1 The four structures across tenses

The tense exercise ebook repeatedly practises **affirmative, negative, interrogative, and negative-interrogative** forms. Instead of memorizing hundreds of separate sentences, learn where the auxiliary goes.

| Form | General idea |
|---|---|
| affirmative | subject + auxiliary if needed + main verb |
| negative | subject + auxiliary + **not** + main verb |
| question | auxiliary + subject + main verb ...? |
| negative question | auxiliary + subject + **not** + main verb ...? / contracted negative auxiliary + subject ...? |

Examples:

**Present simple**

- She works.
- She does not work.
- Does she work?
- Doesn't she work?

**Present continuous**

- She is working.
- She is not working.
- Is she working?
- Isn't she working?

**Present perfect**

- She has worked.
- She has not worked.
- Has she worked?
- Hasn't she worked?

The same movement of the auxiliary works through most English tense forms.

### 16.2 Do not identify tense only from translated endings

The source tense book uses Hindi clues to help beginners recognize forms. They can be useful as a temporary bridge, but English tense choice depends on **meaning and time relationship**, not on matching one Hindi ending to one English tense.

For example:

> I have lived here for five years.

This is present perfect because the situation connects the past to now, not because of a particular translated ending.

### 16.3 Time expressions are clues, not automatic rules

Common clues include:

- present simple: usually, often, every day
- present continuous: now, at the moment, this week
- present perfect: already, yet, just, ever, never, so far
- past simple: yesterday, last week, in 2020
- past continuous: at 8 p.m. yesterday, while
- past perfect: before, by the time
- future perfect: by Friday, by then

Always check the meaning of the whole sentence.

## 17. Present Simple (Present Indefinite)

### Main uses

Use the present simple for:

1. habits and routines: `I wake up at 7.`
2. facts and general truths: `Water boils at 100°C at standard pressure.`
3. permanent or long-term situations: `She lives in Pune.`
4. schedules/timetables: `The train leaves at 6:30.`
5. states: `I understand the problem.`

### Structure

**Affirmative**

> Subject + V1 / V1+s/es

- I work here.
- She works here.

**Negative**

> Subject + do/does not + V1

- I do not work here.
- She does not work here.

**Question**

> Do/Does + subject + V1 ...?

- Do you work here?
- Does she work here?

### Adding -s or -es

- work -> works
- play -> plays
- go -> goes
- watch -> watches
- wash -> washes
- study -> studies

After **does**, use the base form:

- Incorrect: `Does he goes?`
- Correct: `Does he go?`

---

## 18. Present Continuous

Use it for an action happening now, around now, or a temporary situation.

> Subject + am/is/are + V-ing

- I am reading now.
- She is working from home this week.
- They are waiting outside.

**Negative:** `She is not working.`  
**Question:** `Is she working?`

### Spelling

- make -> making
- run -> running
- lie -> lying

### Future arrangement

The present continuous can describe a planned personal arrangement:

> I am meeting the client tomorrow.

### Common error

- Usually unnatural: `I am knowing him.`
- Natural: `I know him.`

---

## 19. Present Perfect

> Subject + has/have + V3

Use it when the past is connected to the present.

### Main uses

**Experience without a finished past time**

> I have visited Delhi three times.

**Recent result**

> She has finished the report.

**Unfinished time period**

> I have had three meetings today.  
> *(Today is not finished.)*

**Situation continuing until now**

> We have known each other for ten years.

### Present perfect vs simple past

Use the simple past with a finished past time:

- Correct: `We visited Europe last year.`
- Incorrect in standard usage: `We have visited Europe last year.`

Use present perfect when no finished past time is given and the experience/result matters now:

> We have visited Europe several times.

**Negative:** `I have not finished.`  
**Question:** `Have you finished?`

---

## 20. Present Perfect Continuous

> Subject + has/have been + V-ing

Use it to emphasize duration or an activity continuing/recently continuing up to now.

- I have been studying for two hours.
- She has been working here since 2022.
- It has been raining all morning.

### Since vs for

- since 9 a.m.
- since Monday
- since 2020
- for two hours
- for three days
- for a long time

### Present perfect or present perfect continuous?

> I have written five emails.  
Focus: completed result/number.

> I have been writing emails all morning.  
Focus: activity/duration.

Some stative verbs normally prefer the perfect simple:

> I have known him for years.

---

## 21. Past Simple (Past Indefinite)

> Subject + V2

Use it for completed past events, past habits, and past states.

- I called her yesterday.
- We lived there for five years.
- He went to school by bus when he was a child.

**Negative**

> Subject + did not + V1

- I did not call her.

**Question**

> Did + subject + V1 ...?

- Did you call her?

After **did**, always use V1:

- Incorrect: `Did you went?`
- Correct: `Did you go?`

---

## 22. Past Continuous

> Subject + was/were + V-ing

Use it for:

- an action in progress at a past time: `At 8 p.m., I was working.`
- background action: `It was raining when we left.`
- two simultaneous actions: `I was cooking while she was setting the table.`

**Negative:** `He was not sleeping.`  
**Question:** `Were they waiting?`

### Past simple + past continuous

> I was driving when my phone rang.

The continuous action was already in progress; the simple-past event interrupted or occurred during it.

---

## 23. Past Perfect

> Subject + had + V3

Use it when you need to show that one past event happened before another past event.

> By the time I arrived, the meeting had started.

Timeline:

1. meeting started
2. I arrived

Do not use past perfect merely because an event happened long ago. Use it when the earlier-past relationship matters.

**Negative:** `She had not left.`  
**Question:** `Had she left?`

---

## 24. Past Perfect Continuous

> Subject + had been + V-ing

Use it to emphasize duration before a past point or event.

- I had been waiting for an hour when the bus arrived.
- She was tired because she had been working all night.

Compare:

> He had repaired the machine.  
Focus: completed result.

> He had been repairing the machine.  
Focus: continuing activity/process.

---

## 25. Future Simple

> Subject + will + V1

Common uses:

- decision made at the moment: `I'll answer the phone.`
- prediction: `I think it will rain.`
- promise: `I will help you.`
- offer: `I'll carry that for you.`

**Negative:** `will not / won't + V1`  
**Question:** `Will + subject + V1 ...?`

### Will vs be going to

Use **be going to** for a prior intention or evidence-based prediction:

- I am going to start a course next month.
- Look at those clouds. It is going to rain.

This distinction is useful, though real usage often overlaps.

---

## 26. Future Continuous

> Subject + will be + V-ing

Use it for an activity that will be in progress at a future time.

- At 10 tomorrow, I will be attending a meeting.
- This time next week, we will be travelling.

It can also make a question about plans sound neutral/polite:

> Will you be using the conference room this afternoon?

---

## 27. Future Perfect

> Subject + will have + V3

Use it for something expected to be completed before a future point.

- By Friday, I will have finished the report.
- By 2030, she will have worked here for ten years.

Look for time markers such as **by**, **by then**, **before**, and **by the time**.

---

## 28. Future Perfect Continuous

> Subject + will have been + V-ing

Use it to emphasize duration up to a future point.

> By December, I will have been working here for five years.

This form is correct but less common in everyday speech than simpler future forms. Use it when duration is important.

---

## 29. Tense Comparison Quick Guide

| Meaning | Natural tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| routine | present simple | I work from home on Fridays. |
| happening now | present continuous | I am working now. |
| present result | present perfect | I have finished the task. |
| duration until now | present perfect continuous | I have been working for two hours. |
| completed past | past simple | I finished yesterday. |
| in progress in past | past continuous | I was working at 8. |
| earlier of two past events | past perfect | I had left before he called. |
| duration before past point | past perfect continuous | I had been waiting for an hour. |
| prediction/promise | future simple | I will help you. |
| future in progress | future continuous | I will be working at 8. |
| complete before future point | future perfect | I will have finished by 8. |
| duration up to future point | future perfect continuous | I will have been working for three hours by 8. |

# Part V - Helping Verbs, Modals, and Questions

## 30. Primary Auxiliary Verbs

The main auxiliary verbs are **be, have,** and **do**.

### Be

Forms: am, is, are, was, were, be, been, being

Uses:

- continuous: `She is working.`
- passive: `The email was sent.`
- linking verb: `He is happy.`

### Have

Forms: have, has, had

Used in perfect forms:

- I have finished.
- She has arrived.
- They had left.

### Do

Forms: do, does, did

Used for questions, negatives, and emphasis in simple tenses:

- Do you know?
- He doesn't know.
- I **do** understand your concern.

---

## 31. Modal Verbs

Modal verbs express ability, possibility, permission, obligation, advice, willingness, prediction, or deduction.

Common modals:

- can
- could
- may
- might
- will
- would
- shall
- should
- must

Related semi-modals include **ought to, have to, need to, used to, be able to**.

### Core grammar rule

> modal + base verb (V1)

- can go
- should study
- must finish
- will call

Not:

- `can goes`
- `should to study`
- `must finished`

### 31.1 Can

Ability:

> I can swim.

Informal permission:

> Can I use your phone?

General possibility:

> Mistakes can happen.

### 31.2 Could

Past/general ability:

> I could swim when I was six.

Polite request:

> Could you send me the file?

Possibility:

> It could rain later.

### 31.3 May and might

Permission, especially formal:

> May I come in?

Possibility:

> It may rain.  
> It might rain.

*Might* often sounds a little less certain, but context matters.

### 31.4 Must and have to

Strong obligation:

> You must wear a helmet.

External/practical necessity:

> I have to submit this before 5 p.m.

Important difference:

- `You must not smoke here.` = it is prohibited.
- `You don't have to come.` = it is not necessary.

### 31.5 Should / ought to

Advice or expectation:

> You should rest.

> The parcel should arrive tomorrow.

### 31.6 Will and would

Future/willingness:

> I will call you.

Polite request:

> Would you please help me?

Hypothetical result:

> I would travel more if I had more time.

Past habit:

> When we were children, we would play outside every evening.

### 31.7 Shall

In modern English, **shall** is much less common than **will**, especially in ordinary American English. It remains useful in formal/legal language and in suggestions with *I/we*:

> Shall we begin?

---


### 31.8 Perfect modals: modal + have + V3

The spoken-English and grammar books include forms such as **could have, might have, should have, must have,** and **ought to have**. These are important, but their meanings are not the same.

| Form | Common meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| could have + V3 | past possibility/opportunity that did not happen, or uncertain possibility | I could have helped, but nobody asked me. |
| may/might have + V3 | possible past event | She might have missed the train. |
| should have + V3 | expected/advisable past action, often with regret or criticism | You should have called me. |
| ought to have + V3 | similar to *should have*; more formal/less common | They ought to have informed us. |
| must have + V3 | strong logical deduction about the past | He must have forgotten the meeting. |
| would have + V3 | hypothetical past result | I would have gone if I had been free. |

Important correction:

> `You must have come.`

This normally sounds like a **deduction** ("I strongly conclude that you came"), not "You had a duty to come." For an unfulfilled past duty, use:

> You **should have come**.

### 31.9 Ability and obligation across time

**Ability**

- present: `I can swim.`
- past general ability: `I could swim when I was five.`
- a specific successful past action often uses `was/were able to`: `We were able to fix the server before midnight.`
- future: `I will be able to join tomorrow.`

**Obligation/necessity**

- present: `I have to leave now.`
- past: `I had to leave early.`
- future: `I will have to leave by 6.`

Natural past question:

> Did you have to work yesterday?

Avoid the old-fashioned or unnatural-for-most-speakers form:

> `Had you to work yesterday?`

### 31.10 Used to, be used to, and get used to

These three patterns are different.

**used to + V1** = a past habit or state that is no longer true.

> I used to wake up late.

Negative/question forms are commonly:

> I didn't use to wake up early.  
> Did you use to live here?

**be used to + noun/-ing** = be accustomed to something.

> I am used to the noise.  
> I am used to working at night.

Not:

> `I am used to work at night.`

**get used to + noun/-ing** = become accustomed to something.

> It took me a week to get used to the new schedule.

### 31.11 Need and dare

**need to** behaves like an ordinary verb:

> I need to leave.  
> She doesn't need to come.  
> Did you need to call him?

**needn't + V1** is a modal-style form meaning "it is not necessary":

> You needn't wait.

**dare** can be an ordinary verb:

> He didn't dare to ask.

A modal-style use also exists, especially in formal or fixed expressions:

> How dare you say that?

For everyday learners, the ordinary-verb pattern `dare to` is usually easiest.


## 32. Building Questions

### 32.1 Yes/no questions with be

> Be + subject ...?

- Are you ready?
- Is he at home?
- Were they late?

### 32.2 Yes/no questions with do

For present/past simple main verbs:

> Do/Does/Did + subject + V1 ...?

- Do you work here?
- Does she drive?
- Did they call?

### 32.3 Questions with auxiliaries/modals

Move the auxiliary/modal before the subject:

- Have you finished?
- Can you help?
- Will she come?
- Should we wait?

### 32.4 WH-question words

| Word | Asks about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| who | person | Who called? |
| whom | object person, formal | Whom did you meet? |
| whose | possession | Whose bag is this? |
| what | thing/information | What happened? |
| which | choice | Which one do you want? |
| when | time | When will you arrive? |
| where | place | Where do you live? |
| why | reason | Why are you late? |
| how | manner/condition | How did you do it? |

Useful **how** combinations:

- how much
- how many
- how long
- how far
- how often
- how old
- how soon
- how fast


### 32.4A Useful WH combinations

| Expression | What it asks about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| how many | countable number | How many people are coming? |
| how much | amount/price | How much water do you need? |
| how long | duration/length | How long have you lived here? |
| how often | frequency | How often do you exercise? |
| how far | distance | How far is the station? |
| how old | age | How old is your brother? |
| what kind of | type/category | What kind of music do you like? |
| what time / at what time | clock time | What time does the meeting start? |
| who ... with | person accompanying | Who are you going with? |
| from where / where ... from | origin | Where are you coming from? |

In natural modern English, `Who are you going with?` is usually more conversational than `With whom are you going?`, although the second is correct and more formal.

**How come ...?** is an informal way to ask "why":

> How come you're late?

Notice that it normally uses statement word order after *how come*:

> How come **you are** late?

not normally:

> `How come are you late?`

**Whence** means "from where" but is old-fashioned or literary. Beginners do not need it for normal conversation.


### 32.5 Subject questions

When **who/what** is the subject, do not normally use *do/does/did*:

> Who called you?

Not:

> Who did call you?  
*(Possible only for special emphasis.)*

### 32.6 Indirect questions

Indirect questions are more polite and use statement word order after the introductory phrase:

- Direct: `Where is the station?`
- Indirect: `Could you tell me where the station is?`

Not:

> Could you tell me where is the station?

## 33. Negatives

Place **not** after the first auxiliary/modal:

- She is **not** coming.
- I have **not** finished.
- We will **not** wait.
- You should **not** worry.

With simple present/past lexical verbs, use *do*:

- I do not know.
- He does not work here.
- They did not call.

Common contractions:

- isn't, aren't
- wasn't, weren't
- don't, doesn't, didn't
- haven't, hasn't, hadn't
- won't
- wouldn't
- can't
- couldn't
- shouldn't

---

## 34. Question Tags

A question tag is a short question added to a statement.

Positive statement -> negative tag:

> You are ready, **aren't you?**

Negative statement -> positive tag:

> She isn't coming, **is she?**

Use the same auxiliary/modal where possible:

- He can swim, can't he?
- They have finished, haven't they?
- She went home, didn't she?

Special common form:

> I'm late, **aren't I?**

# Part VI - Voice, Reported Speech, Comparison, and Clauses

## 35. Active and Passive Voice

### 35.1 Meaning

**Active voice** focuses on the doer:

> The technician repaired the server.

**Passive voice** focuses on the receiver/result:

> The server was repaired by the technician.

### 35.2 Basic passive formula

> appropriate form of **be** + V3

The object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive sentence.

### 35.3 Common passive forms

| Active tense | Passive pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| present simple | am/is/are + V3 | The report is checked. |
| present continuous | am/is/are being + V3 | The report is being checked. |
| present perfect | has/have been + V3 | The report has been checked. |
| past simple | was/were + V3 | The report was checked. |
| past continuous | was/were being + V3 | The report was being checked. |
| past perfect | had been + V3 | The report had been checked. |
| future simple | will be + V3 | The report will be checked. |
| modal | modal + be + V3 | The report must be checked. |


### 35.3A Additional passive patterns and limits

The grammar source also includes the future perfect passive and gives useful warnings about forms that are normally avoided.

| Form | Passive pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| future perfect | will have been + V3 | The report will have been completed by Friday. |
| modal perfect | modal + have been + V3 | The payment should have been processed yesterday. |

#### Not every tense is normally made passive

Forms such as these are theoretically possible in special contexts but are normally avoided because they become extremely heavy:

- present perfect continuous passive;
- past perfect continuous passive;
- future continuous passive;
- future perfect continuous passive.

Instead of forcing a form such as:

> `The bridge has been being repaired for two months.`

a natural writer often chooses:

> Workers have been repairing the bridge for two months.

or:

> The bridge has been under repair for two months.

#### Intransitive verbs cannot normally form a passive

A verb needs an object that can become the passive subject.

> The baby slept.

There is no object, so there is no normal passive form.

Similarly, do not force passive forms from verbs such as *arrive, happen,* or *die* when they are used intransitively.

#### Verbs with two objects

Active:

> She gave **me** **a book**.

Two passive versions may be possible:

> I was given a book.

> A book was given to me.

The first is often more natural when the person is important.

#### Imperatives and passive meaning

Traditional grammar books often teach a structure such as:

> Let the door be closed.

This is grammatical but formal. In ordinary English, choose the clearest instruction:

> Close the door.

If the person who performs the action is unimportant:

> Please make sure the door is closed.


### 35.4 When passive voice is useful

Use it when:

- the doer is unknown: `My wallet was stolen.`
- the doer is obvious/unimportant: `The road was closed.`
- the result/process matters more: `The invoice has been approved.`
- formal/process writing needs object focus: `The samples were tested.`

### 35.5 When passive voice is weak

Do not use passive just to sound formal.

Wordy:

> The decision was made by the team.

Usually stronger:

> The team made the decision.

### 35.6 Correcting problematic passive examples

Source-like sentence:

> `He does not teaches me English.`

Correct active:

> He does not teach me English.

Correct passive:

> I am not taught English by him.  
This is grammatical but less natural than the active form in normal conversation.

Source-like sentence:

> `Ram is talking with Mohan.`

Do **not** force this into an unnatural passive such as `Mohan is being talked with Ram.` The natural sentence is simply:

> Ram is talking with Mohan.

## 36. Direct and Indirect (Reported) Speech

### 36.1 Direct speech

Exact words are quoted:

> Riya said, "I am tired."

### 36.2 Reported speech

We report the message:

> Riya said that she was tired.

### 36.3 Common tense backshift

When the reporting verb is in the past and the situation is viewed from that past perspective, common changes are:

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| present simple | past simple |
| present continuous | past continuous |
| present perfect | past perfect |
| present perfect continuous | past perfect continuous |
| past simple | past perfect, often |
| past continuous | past perfect continuous, often |
| will | would |
| can | could |
| may | might |

Example:

> He said, "I am busy."  
> He said that he was busy.

### 36.4 Backshift is not automatic in every real sentence

If a fact is still true, speakers often keep the present:

> The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.


### 36.4A Reporting-verb tense and pronoun perspective

A useful source rule is: when the reporting verb is **present or future**, a tense backshift is normally unnecessary.

> She says, "I am tired."  
> She says that she is tired.

> He will say, "I need more time."  
> He will say that he needs more time.

When the reporting verb is past, backshift is common, but real English still depends on whether the information is being presented as current or past.

#### Pronouns change according to who is speaking to whom

Do not change pronouns mechanically. Work out the real people involved.

Direct:

> Maya said to me, "I will help you."

Reported:

> Maya told me that **she** would help **me**.

Here:

- `I` referred to Maya -> `she`;
- `you` referred to me -> `me`.

This is clearer than memorizing a rule without understanding the speakers.

#### Modals that often remain unchanged

**could, should, would,** and **might** often stay the same in reported speech:

> He said, "I could help."  
> He said that he could help.

> She said, "You should rest."  
> She said that I should rest.


### 36.5 Time/place changes

Depending on context:

- now -> then
- today -> that day
- yesterday -> the previous day / the day before
- tomorrow -> the next day / the following day
- here -> there
- this -> that
- these -> those

### 36.6 Say vs tell

- say + something: `He said that he was busy.`
- say + something + to someone: `He said hello to me.`
- tell + person + something: `He told me that he was busy.`

Not:

> He told that he was busy.

### 36.7 Reported questions

Direct:

> She asked, "Where do you live?"

Reported:

> She asked where I lived.

Use statement word order; do not keep question inversion:

- Correct: `She asked where I lived.`
- Incorrect: `She asked where did I live.`

### 36.8 Reported commands/requests

> He said, "Please sit down."  
> He asked me to sit down.

> She said, "Don't touch it."  
> She told me not to touch it.

## 37. Degrees of Comparison

### Positive, comparative, superlative

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| tall | taller | tallest |
| small | smaller | smallest |
| happy | happier | happiest |
| beautiful | more beautiful | most beautiful |
| good | better | best |
| bad | worse | worst |
| far | farther/further | farthest/furthest |

### Main patterns

> Riya is tall.  
> Riya is taller than Neha.  
> Riya is the tallest student in the group.


### Comparison spelling and word-choice rules

**Short adjectives** often take `-er/-est`:

- tall -> taller -> tallest
- small -> smaller -> smallest

**Adjectives ending in consonant + y** usually change `y` to `i`:

- happy -> happier -> happiest
- easy -> easier -> easiest

**Many short consonant-vowel-consonant adjectives** double the final consonant:

- big -> bigger -> biggest
- hot -> hotter -> hottest

**Longer adjectives** commonly use `more/most`:

- beautiful -> more beautiful -> most beautiful
- important -> more important -> most important

Do not combine both systems:

- Incorrect: `more easier`
- Correct: `easier`

Important irregular forms:

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| good | better | best |
| bad | worse | worst |
| many/much | more | most |
| little (amount) | less | least |
| far | farther/further | farthest/furthest |

`farther` is often used for physical distance; `further` is common for both physical and figurative extension, especially in British/international English.


### 37.1 Transforming the degree without changing the basic comparison

Traditional grammar exercises often ask you to express a similar comparison in different forms. The wording must preserve the original meaning.

**One person/thing compared with all others in a group**

> Superlative: Maya is **the tallest** student in the class.  
> Comparative: Maya is **taller than any other** student in the class.  
> Positive: **No other** student in the class is **as tall as** Maya.

**One member of a small top group**

> Superlative-style: This is **one of the most useful** tools in the set.  
> Comparative: This tool is **more useful than most other** tools in the set.  
> Positive: **Very few** tools in the set are **as useful as** this one.

Be careful: these transformations are valid only when the logic really matches. `One of the best` does **not** mean `the best`.

### As ... as

> This phone is as fast as that one.

Negative:

> This phone is not as expensive as that one.

### Common errors

- Incorrect: `more better`
- Correct: `better`

- Incorrect: `He is tallest in class.`
- Correct: `He is the tallest in the class.`

## 38. Sentence Types

### By purpose

- **Declarative:** `I work here.`
- **Interrogative:** `Do you work here?`
- **Imperative:** `Please sit down.`
- **Exclamatory:** `What a beautiful view!`

### By clause structure

**Simple sentence:** one independent clause.

> I finished the report.

**Compound sentence:** two independent clauses joined appropriately.

> I finished the report, and I sent it to my manager.

**Complex sentence:** independent clause + dependent clause.

> I sent the report after I checked it.

**Compound-complex sentence:** at least two independent clauses plus at least one dependent clause.

> I checked the report, and I sent it after my manager approved the figures.

---

## 39. Clauses and Relative Clauses

An **independent clause** can stand alone:

> The server is running.

A **dependent clause** cannot normally stand alone:

> because the server is running

### Relative clauses

Relative clauses describe a noun.

- The woman **who called me** is the recruiter.
- The laptop **that I bought** is fast.
- The office **where I work** is nearby.
- The colleague **whose desk is next to mine** is on leave.

**Who** usually refers to people.  
**Which** refers to things.  
**That** can refer to people or things in many defining clauses.

### Defining vs non-defining

Defining information identifies the noun:

> Employees who work remotely must use the VPN.

Non-defining information adds extra information and is separated by commas:

> Amit, who works in Finance, will join us later.

---

## 40. Conditionals

Conditionals express results that depend on conditions.

### Zero conditional - general truth

> If + present, present

> If you heat ice, it melts.

### First conditional - real future possibility

> If + present, will + V1

> If it rains, we will stay home.

### Second conditional - hypothetical/unlikely present or future

> If + past, would + V1

> If I had more time, I would learn Spanish.

For formal/hypothetical style, **were** is often used for all persons:

> If I were you, I would accept the offer.

### Third conditional - unreal past

> If + had + V3, would have + V3

> If I had studied harder, I would have passed.

### Mixed conditional

Past condition with present result:

> If I had taken that job, I would be living in Bengaluru now.

---

## 41. Gerunds and Infinitives

### Gerund

An **-ing** form used like a noun:

> **Reading** helps me relax.

After many verbs/prepositions:

- enjoy reading
- avoid making
- finish writing
- interested in learning

### To-infinitive

> to + base verb

- want to learn
- need to leave
- decide to apply
- hope to see

### Verb + object + infinitive

- ask someone to do
- tell someone to do
- want someone to do

> I asked him to wait.

### Meaning changes

> I stopped **smoking**. = I ended the habit.  
> I stopped **to smoke**. = I paused another activity in order to smoke.

> I remembered **locking** the door. = memory of a completed action.  
> I remembered **to lock** the door. = I did not forget the required action.

---

## 42. Imperatives, Requests, and Suggestions

### Imperative

Use the base verb:

- Sit down.
- Please wait here.
- Don't touch that.

### Polite request scale

Informal/direct:

> Can you help me?

Polite:

> Could you help me, please?

More formal:

> Would you mind helping me?

With **would you mind**, use **-ing**:

> Would you mind closing the door?

### Suggestions

- Let's take a break.
- Shall we start?
- Why don't we try again?
- How about meeting tomorrow?
- We could ask the manager.

---

## 43. Punctuation and Capitalization

### Capital letters

Use capitals for:

- first word of a sentence;
- names: *Rahul, India, Microsoft*;
- days and months: *Monday, August*;
- languages and nationalities: *English, Indian*;
- the pronoun **I**.

Corrected source-style example:

- Incorrect: `May i know the reason ?`
- Correct: `May I know the reason?`

### Full stop/period

> The meeting starts at 10.

### Question mark

> Where are you going?

Do not normally put a space before `?` in English.

### Comma

Useful for:

- list items: `We bought tea, coffee, and sugar.`
- introductory clauses: `After the meeting, I went home.`
- non-defining information: `Riya, my manager, called me.`
- joining two independent clauses with a coordinator: `I called him, but he did not answer.`

### Apostrophe

Contraction:

> don't = do not

Possession:

> Rahul's laptop

Do not use an apostrophe merely to make a regular plural:

- Incorrect: `book's` for plural
- Correct: `books`

### Colon and semicolon

Colon introduces or explains:

> Bring three things: a pen, a notebook, and your ID.

Semicolon can connect closely related independent clauses:

> The server is down; the team is investigating.

For beginners, two clear sentences are usually safer.

# Part VII - Spoken English and Natural Conversation

## 44. How to Become More Fluent

Fluency is the ability to communicate smoothly enough for the situation. It does **not** mean speaking extremely fast or using difficult words.

### Build fluency through chunks

Learn groups of words instead of isolated words:

- as far as I know
- give me a moment
- I don't think so
- it depends on
- could you repeat that?
- I'm not sure, but...

### Think in small English sentences

Instead of translating a long sentence from your first language, start with short ideas:

> I reached the office late. There was heavy traffic. I had a meeting at ten, so I called my manager on the way.

This is better practice than trying to produce one very complicated sentence.

### The fluency loop

1. Listen to natural English.
2. Notice useful sentence chunks.
3. Repeat them aloud.
4. Change one or two words.
5. Use them in your own situation.
6. Record yourself.
7. Correct one or two recurring problems, not everything at once.

## 45. Greetings and Introductions

### Neutral greetings

- Hello.
- Hi.
- Good morning.
- Good afternoon.
- Good evening.
- Nice to meet you.
- It's good to see you again.

**Good night** is normally used when leaving or going to sleep, not as the first greeting of an evening meeting.

### Asking how someone is

- How are you?
- How are things?
- How have you been?
- How's it going? *(informal)*

Responses:

- I'm fine, thank you. How about you?
- I'm doing well, thanks.
- Pretty good.
- I've been busy, but I'm doing well.

The source conversation opener `How do you do?` is grammatically correct but formal and old-fashioned in many modern contexts. `Nice to meet you` or `How are you?` is more natural for most learners.

### Introducing yourself

> Hi, I'm Arjun. I work as a software engineer. I mainly work on web applications. Nice to meet you.

### Introducing another person

> Priya, this is Sameer from our Finance team. Sameer, Priya is our project manager.

## 46. Politeness: Please, Thank You, and Responses

### Requests

- Please send me the file.
- Could you send me the file, please?
- Would you please check this once?
- Could you give me a moment?

**Kindly** is common in South Asian business English, but repeated use can sound overly formal or commanding. Often **please** is warmer and more natural.

### Thanking

- Thank you.
- Thanks a lot.
- Thank you for your help.
- I really appreciate it.
- Thanks for letting me know.

### Responding to thanks

Natural:

- You're welcome.
- My pleasure.
- No problem. *(informal)*
- Happy to help.

`Mention not` appears in some older learning materials but is uncommon in modern everyday English.

## 47. Asking for Repetition or Clarification

Useful natural phrases:

- Sorry, could you repeat that?
- I didn't catch that.
- Could you say that again, please?
- Could you speak a little more slowly?
- What do you mean by ...?
- Could you explain that in another way?
- Do you mean that ...?
- Let me make sure I understood correctly.

`I beg your pardon?` is correct but can sound formal depending on the context.

### Happiness

- I'm really happy about it.
- That's great!
- I'm glad to hear that.
- What wonderful news!

### Surprise

- That's surprising.

### Agreement

## 49. Conversation Fillers and Thinking Time

Fillers can help you hold the floor while thinking, but too many make speech weak.

Useful:

- Well, ...
- Let me think.
- Actually, ...
- In my opinion, ...
- As far as I know, ...
- The main point is ...
- What I mean is ...

- On the other hand, ...

## 50. High-Value Daily-Use Sentence Patterns

The daily-sentence ebook contains thousands of Hindi-to-English examples. A more effective way to master them is to learn **reusable patterns** that can produce many new sentences.

### 50.1 Don't + base verb

Use for negative commands.

- Don't lie to me.
- Don't hesitate.
- Don't worry.
- Don't interrupt me.
- Don't make the same mistake again.
- Don't take it personally.

### 50.2 Please / Could you + base verb

- Please wait here.
- Could you open the door?
- Could you send it again?
- Please let me know.

### 50.3 Try to + base verb

- Try to understand.
- Try to stay calm.
- Try to finish it today.

### 50.4 You should + base verb

- You should rest.
- You should be careful.
- You should check the details first.

Source example retained:

> You should be ashamed.

This is grammatical but strong and potentially insulting. Use it only when such a strong judgment is intended.

### 50.5 I am + adjective / V-ing

- I am tired.
- I am busy.
- I am kidding.
- I am waiting for you.
- I am working on it.

### 50.6 I want / need / have to

- I want to talk to you.
- I need some help.
- I have to leave now.
- I don't want to argue.

### 50.7 Let me + base verb

- Let me check.
- Let me explain.
- Let me think.
- Let me handle this.

### 50.8 Let + person + base verb

- Let him speak.
- Let her decide.
- Let them finish.

### 50.9 May I / Can I / Could I

- May I know the reason?
- Can I sit here?
- Could I ask you something?

### 50.10 Do you mind ...?

> Do you mind + V-ing ...?

- Do you mind waiting for five minutes?
- Do you mind closing the window?

If someone asks `Do you mind if I sit here?`, a natural response meaning permission is `No, not at all.`

### 50.11 There is / There are

- There is a problem.
- There is no need to worry.
- There are two options.
- Is there anything else?

### 50.12 It is + adjective + to-infinitive

- It is difficult to explain.
- It is important to practise every day.
- It is better to ask than to assume.

### 50.13 It takes / It took / It will take

Use this pattern for the time needed to complete something.

> It + take + person + time + to + V1

- It takes ten minutes to get there.
- It takes me twenty minutes to get ready.
- It took me two hours to finish the task.
- It will take a few days to receive the result.

Do not say `It takes two hours for finish.` Say `It takes two hours to finish.`

### 50.14 I think / I don't think

- I think you're right.
- I don't think that's a good idea.
- I think we should wait.

### 50.15 I have no idea / I'm not sure

- I have no idea.
- I'm not sure what happened.
- I'm not sure whether he will come.

### 50.16 It depends on

- It depends on the situation.
- It depends on how much time we have.

### 50.17 What happened / What's wrong?

- What happened?
- What's wrong?
- What's the matter?
- Is everything okay?

### 50.18 That's + adjective/noun phrase

- That's great.
- That's enough.
- That's not fair.
- That's a good idea.
- That's more like it.

For the Hindi idea sometimes translated as `Hurry!` in phrase books, `That's more like it!` may be the natural English expression when the intended meaning is “Now that's better.”

### 50.19 I didn't mean to

- I didn't mean to hurt you.
- I didn't mean to interrupt.
- I didn't mean to sound rude.

### 50.20 I would rather

> would rather + V1

- I'd rather stay home.
- I'd rather not discuss it now.

### 50.21 Used to

> used to + V1

Past habit/state that is no longer true:

- I used to play cricket every day.
- We used to live in Delhi.

Do not confuse it with **be used to + noun/-ing**, meaning “be accustomed to”:

- I am used to working late.

### 50.22 Be supposed to

- I'm supposed to submit this today.
- You are supposed to wear an ID card.

### 50.23 Have you ever ...?

- Have you ever visited Goa?
- Have you ever worked with this software?

### 50.24 How long have you ...?

- How long have you lived here?
- How long have you been learning English?

### 50.25 Would you like ...?

- Would you like some tea?
- Would you like to join us?

### 50.26 How many, how much, and how often

Use **how many** with countable nouns and **how much** with uncountable nouns.

- How many people are coming?
- How many times did you call?
- How much water do you need?
- How much time do we have?

Use **how often** to ask about frequency.

- How often do you exercise?
- How often does this problem occur?

### 50.28 I wish ...

Use **wish** when reality is different from what you want.

**Wish about the present:** `wish + past simple`

- I wish I had more free time. *(I do not have enough free time now.)*
- I wish I were there. *(I am not there now.)*

`Were` is traditional and common after *wish* for all subjects in careful English. `Was` is also heard informally with *I/he/she*.

**Wish about a past event:** `wish + had + V3`

- I wish I had studied harder. *(I did not study hard enough.)*
- She wishes she had accepted the offer.

**Wish about ability or a possible change:** `wish + could`

- I wish I could speak more confidently.
- I wish we could meet this week.

### 50.29 Prefer A to B

Use **prefer ... to ...**, not normally `prefer ... than ...`.

- I prefer tea to coffee.
- He prefers French to German.
- I prefer walking to cycling.

For a specific choice now, **would rather** is also useful:

- I'd rather walk than take a taxi.

### 50.30 Worth and worthwhile

**worth + noun/-ing**

- The book is worth reading.
- The idea is worth considering.
- It isn't worth the risk.

**worthwhile + noun/-ing/to-infinitive**

- It was a worthwhile discussion.
- It is worthwhile learning these patterns.
- It is worthwhile to check the details first.

Avoid `worth to read`; use `worth reading`.

### 50.31 Make, let, and help + object + verb

- The joke **made me laugh**.
- Let **him speak**.
- She **helped me understand** the process.

After **make** and **let** in the active voice, use the base verb without *to*.

> make/let + object + V1

With **help**, both `help someone do` and `help someone to do` are possible.

### 50.32 See/hear + object + V1 or V-ing

These patterns describe perception.

- I saw him **cross** the road. *(I saw the whole action.)*
- I saw him **crossing** the road. *(I saw the action in progress.)*
- I heard the baby **crying**.

The exact difference depends on whether you focus on the complete event or an event in progress.

### 50.33 Be born in / on / at

Use:

- **in** + month/year/place: `I was born in January.` / `I was born in Pune.`
- **on** + date/day: `She was born on 12 May.`
- **at** + clock time: `He was born at 2:00 a.m.`

The verb is normally passive in form: `was/were born`.

### 50.34 On behalf of

**on behalf of someone** means acting or speaking for that person or group.

- I apologized on behalf of the team.
- She accepted the award on behalf of her father.

### 50.35 Via, ago, by, and after a while

- **via** = through/by way of: `We flew to New York via Dubai.`
- **ago** = before now, used with a past time: `I met him two days ago.`
- **by Friday** = no later than Friday: `Please send it by Friday.`
- **after a while** = after some time: `I'll come back after a while.`
- **all the while** = throughout that period: `All the while, I was waiting for a reply.`

### 50.36 Upside down, right side up, and inside out

These are practical orientation expressions.

- The picture is upside down.
- Turn the bottle right side up.
- You're wearing your T-shirt inside out.

Do not confuse **inside out** (inner surface is outside) with **upside down** (top and bottom are reversed).

### 50.37 Know how to + V1

Use this pattern for practical knowledge or ability.

- Do you know how to drive?
- She knows how to use the software.
- I don't know how to cook this dish.

### 50.38 Could have + V3

**could have + V3** usually talks about a past possibility or an opportunity/ability that was not used.

- You could have called me. *(Calling was possible, but you did not call.)*
- Anything could have happened. *(It was possible.)*

Do not confuse this with `could have + noun`, where **have** is the main verb meaning possess:

- At that salary, he could have a larger apartment. *(possible possession)*

### 50.39 Call on

**call on someone** can mean *visit someone*, especially in formal or older usage.

- I called on my uncle yesterday.

In everyday modern English, `visit` or `go and see` is often clearer:

- I'm going to visit my uncle tomorrow.

### 50.40 Needless to say

**Needless to say, ...** introduces something the speaker thinks is obvious.

- Needless to say, safety comes first.

Use it carefully: if the point is not actually obvious, the phrase can sound dismissive.

## 51. Important Corrections to Common Daily-English Errors

| Less natural / incorrect | Better English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You are taking me wrong. | You are misunderstanding me. / You have misunderstood me. | Standard expression |
| May i know the reason ? | May I know the reason? | Capitalize *I*; no space before `?` |
| What a news! | What great news! | *News* is uncountable |
| Finger crossed. | Fingers crossed. | Fixed expression is plural |
| I am having a car. | I have a car. | Possession *have* is normally non-continuous |
| I am knowing him. | I know him. | *Know* is normally stative |
| He does not teaches. | He does not teach. | After *does*, use V1 |
| I didn't went. | I didn't go. | After *did*, use V1 |
| Discuss about it. | Discuss it. | *Discuss* takes a direct object |
| Return back. | Return. / Come back. | *Return* already includes “back” |
| Revert back to me. | Reply to me. / Get back to me. | Clearer modern business English |
| I have a doubt. | I have a question. | Often more natural for clarification |
| What is your good name? | What is your name? | More standard internationally |
| Myself Rahul. | I'm Rahul. / My name is Rahul. | *Myself* is not a normal introduction subject |
| I belong to Mumbai. | I'm from Mumbai. / I live in Mumbai. | More natural for origin/residence |
| Pass out from college | Graduate from college | *Pass out* commonly means faint |
| Prepone the meeting | Move the meeting earlier. | More internationally understood |
| Give an exam | Take/sit an exam | Learners take/sit an exam |
| Out of station | Out of town / away from the office | More internationally natural |
| Do the needful | Please take the necessary action. | More specific |
| Kindly revert | Please reply. | Simpler and clearer |

Some expressions in the first column are common in Indian English. The alternatives prioritize broadly understood international English rather than treating every regional form as inherently invalid.

## 52. Practical Conversation Scenarios

The conversation ebook covers situations such as examinations, careers, shopping, health, travel, family life, and everyday interactions. Learn the **communication function** behind each dialogue, not only the exact words.

### 52.1 Two friends discussing exam preparation

**A:** How is your exam preparation going?  
**B:** Pretty well, but I'm still weak in English.  
**A:** Which area are you focusing on?  
**B:** Grammar and writing. I'm also revising the other subjects.  
**A:** Are you using any extra books?  
**B:** Yes, but I'm focusing mainly on my textbooks and practice papers.  
**A:** That sounds sensible. Good luck!  
**B:** Thanks. You too.

Useful pattern: `How is ... going?` asks about progress.

### 52.2 Shopkeeper and customer

**Customer:** Excuse me, how much is this shirt?  
**Shopkeeper:** It's ₹1,200.  
**Customer:** Do you have it in a smaller size?  
**Shopkeeper:** Yes. Would you like to try it on?  
**Customer:** Yes, please. Is there any discount?  
**Shopkeeper:** I can give you ten percent off.  
**Customer:** Great. I'll take it.

Useful patterns: `Do you have this in ...?`, `Can I try it on?`, `I'll take it.`

### 52.3 Doctor and patient - language practice

**Doctor:** What seems to be the problem?  
**Patient:** I've had a sore throat and fever since yesterday.  
**Doctor:** Do you have a cough?  
**Patient:** A little.  
**Doctor:** I'll examine you first.

Notice the present perfect for a recent condition continuing until now. This dialogue is language practice, not medical advice.

### 52.4 Meeting after a long time

**A:** Hey! It's been a long time. How have you been?  
**B:** I've been good. Work has kept me busy. How about you?  
**A:** I'm doing well. We should catch up sometime.  
**B:** Definitely. Let's plan something this weekend.

### 52.5 Asking for directions

**A:** Excuse me, could you tell me how to get to the railway station?  
**B:** Sure. Go straight for about 500 metres, then turn left at the signal.  
**A:** Is it far from there?  
**B:** No, it's about a five-minute walk.  
**A:** Thank you.  
**B:** You're welcome.

### 52.6 Workplace clarification

**A:** Could you clarify one point in the requirement?  
**B:** Sure. Which part is unclear?  
**A:** Should this validation happen before or after submission?  
**B:** Before submission.  
**A:** Got it. Thanks for confirming.

### 52.7 Polite disagreement

**A:** I think we should release today.  
**B:** I understand the urgency, but I'm concerned about the unresolved defect.  
**A:** What do you suggest?  
**B:** I'd prefer to fix it first and release tomorrow morning.  
**A:** That sounds reasonable.

### Dialogue practice method

1. Read both roles aloud.
2. Repeat without looking.
3. Replace names, places, objects, and reasons.
4. Reverse roles.
5. Create a different ending.
6. Record yourself and listen for hesitation and unclear pronunciation.

# Part VIII - Vocabulary Building

## 53. Learn Vocabulary as a System

The provided vocabulary, root-word, synonym-antonym, one-word-substitution, idiom, and verb-form ebooks contain thousands of entries. Simply copying every entry would produce a dictionary rather than an effective handbook. This master preserves their learning value by organizing vocabulary into systems and high-value reference banks.

For each useful word, learn:

1. meaning;
2. pronunciation;
3. part of speech;
4. common collocations;
5. one natural example;
6. related word forms;
7. a synonym/antonym where useful;
8. register: formal, informal, neutral, old-fashioned, or specialized.

Example:

**decide** *(verb)* - choose after considering options  
**decision** *(noun)*  
**decisive** *(adjective)*  
**indecisive** *(adjective)*  
Collocations: *make a decision, final decision, difficult decision*  
Example: `We need to make a decision by Friday.`

### The four-question vocabulary test

Before considering a word “learned,” ask:

- Can I understand it when I read/hear it?

## 56. Synonyms and Antonyms

A **synonym** is a word with the same or similar meaning in some contexts. An **antonym** expresses an opposite meaning.

Synonyms are rarely interchangeable everywhere.

Example:

- *big* and *large* often overlap.
- `a big problem` and `a large problem` are both possible.
- `my big brother` can mean my older brother; `my large brother` means physically large.

### Similar does not mean identical

**effective** = achieves the intended result.  
**efficient** = achieves a result with little wasted time, effort, or resources.

> The method is effective but not efficient.

### Verify lists in context

### 56.1 How to study synonym-antonym entries

The synonym/antonym source often gives a **word, pronunciation, part of speech, meaning, synonyms, antonyms, and a sentence**. That is a good study format, but each field must be checked in context.

For example:

**integrity** *(noun)* - honesty and strong moral principles; also the state of being whole or undivided  
Synonyms in the moral sense: *honesty, uprightness*  
Possible opposite in the moral sense: *dishonesty*  
Example: `A good auditor must act with integrity.`

Notice that a word can have more than one meaning, so one antonym may not work for every sense.

- `every nook and cranny`, rather than `nook and corner` in standard modern usage
- `red tape`, rather than the less common `red tapism`
- `Fingers crossed`, not `Finger crossed`

## 62. Pronunciation and Speaking Clarity

Perfect accent imitation is not necessary. Aim for **clear, understandable speech**.

### 62.1 Final sounds matter

Do not swallow important endings:

- work / works / worked
- ask / asked
- want / wanted
- book / books

These endings often carry grammar information.

### 62.2 -ed endings

Regular past `-ed` has three common pronunciations:

- /t/: worked, watched
- /d/: played, cleaned
- /ɪd/: wanted, needed

The choice depends on the final sound of the base verb, not simply the spelling.

### 62.3 Plural/third-person -s

Three common sounds:

- /s/: books, works
- /z/: bags, runs
- /ɪz/: buses, watches

### 62.4 Word stress

Wrong stress can make a familiar word hard to understand. Notice the strongest syllable:

- `PREsent` (noun/adjective) vs `preSENT` (verb)
- `REcord` (noun) vs `reCORD` (verb)

### 62.5 Sentence stress

Content words are often stressed more strongly:

> I **SENT** the **FILE** to **RAHUL** yesterday.

Function words such as articles and auxiliaries may be reduced in natural speech.

### 62.6 Connected speech

Natural speech joins words. Do not try to speak each word as if it were isolated. Practise whole chunks:

- What do you think?
- Could you help me?
- I have to go.
- Let me know.

### 62.7 Tongue twisters

Tongue twisters are useful for articulation, not as the main path to fluency.

- She sells seashells by the seashore.
- Red lorry, yellow lorry.
- Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers.

Start slowly. Accuracy comes before speed.

### 62.8 Shadowing practice

1. Play a short clip from a clear English speaker.
2. Listen once for meaning.
3. Replay one sentence.
4. Copy rhythm, pauses, stress, and intonation.
5. Record yourself.
6. Compare and repeat.

### 62.9 Similar-sounding word pairs

The spoken-English source includes pairs that learners may confuse. Practise them in short sentences instead of memorizing isolated spellings.

| Pair | Meaning contrast |
|---|---|
| weak / week | not strong / seven days |
| reach / rich | arrive at / wealthy |
| bed / bad | place to sleep / not good |
| warm / worm | moderately hot / small crawling animal |
| letter / later / latter | written character/message / after now / second of two |
| soap / shop | cleaning product / store |
| sweet / sweat | sugary / moisture from skin |
| confident / confidant | sure of yourself / trusted person |
| heroin / heroine | illegal opioid drug / female hero |
| deference / difference | respectful submission / lack of sameness |

Pronunciation varies by accent. The goal is to hear and produce a **clear contrast**, not to copy one accent perfectly.

# Part IX - Writing Better English

## 63. Email Writing

Email writing is not only grammar. A good email is **clear, appropriate, easy to act on, and suitable for the relationship**.

### 63.1 Before writing, ask four questions

A useful planning model from the email material is the **4Rs**:

1. **Reader** - Who will read this?
2. **Response** - What do I want the reader to do or know?
3. **Reaction** - How do I want the reader to feel?
4. **Results** - What outcome should this email produce?

This prevents you from writing a long email that has no clear purpose.

### 63.2 Basic email structure

1. Subject line
2. Greeting/salutation
3. Opening sentence
4. Main purpose/details
5. Requested action or next step
6. Closing sentence
7. Sign-off
8. Signature

### 63.3 Subject line

Make the subject specific enough that the reader understands the purpose.

Weak:

> Update

Better:

> Project Alpha - Testing Status for 17 August

Weak:

> Need Help

Better:

> Help Needed: Access to UAT Server

### 63.4 Greetings

Formal/neutral:

- Dear Mr. Khan,
- Dear Ms. Mehta,
- Hello Priya,
- Hi Sameer,

If you do not know the person's name:

- Dear Hiring Manager,
- Hello Support Team,

`Dear Sir/Madam` is still used but can sound impersonal when a name or role is available.

### 63.5 Opening lines

When replying:

- Thank you for your email.
- Thanks for the update.
- Thank you for sharing the details.

When starting a request:

- I'm writing to request access to ...
- Could you please help me with ...?
- I'd like to confirm ...

Avoid empty formal phrases when they add nothing:

- `Please be informed that ...`
- `Kindly be advised that ...`
- `Please find attached herewith ...`

Clearer:

- `The meeting has been moved to 3 p.m.`
- `I've attached the report.`

### 63.6 State the purpose early

Do not make the reader search for the point.

Wordy:

> With reference to our previous discussion and in continuation of the same, I would like to inform you that we have now completed the testing activity.

Clear:

> We have completed testing for the new release.

### 63.7 Use verbs instead of heavy noun phrases

Heavy noun style is called **nominalisation**.

| Heavy noun phrase | Clear verb form |
|---|---|
| make a decision | decide |
| conduct an investigation | investigate |
| provide clarification | clarify |
| make an improvement | improve |
| provide assistance | help/assist |
| give consideration to | consider |
| reach an agreement | agree |
| make a recommendation | recommend |

Example:

> We conducted an investigation of the issue.  
> We investigated the issue.

The second is shorter and more direct.

### 63.8 KISS - Keep It Short and Simple

Prefer familiar words unless a technical term is necessary.

| Wordy/formal | Simpler |
|---|---|
| commence | start/begin |
| utilise | use |
| require | need |
| terminate | end |
| state | say |
| inform | tell |
| visualise | see/imagine |
| assist | help |
| sufficient | enough |
| purchase | buy |
| dispatch | send |
| endeavour | try |
| with regard to | about |
| at the present moment | now |
| in the event that | if |
| in view of the fact that | because/since |
| at a later date | later |

Simple does **not** mean childish. It means the reader should not have to decode your sentence.

### 63.9 Use active voice when it improves clarity

Passive:

> The document will be reviewed by our team tomorrow.

Active:

> Our team will review the document tomorrow.

Passive remains useful when the actor is unknown or unimportant:

> The payment was processed yesterday.

### 63.10 Tone

Tone is the attitude your writing communicates. The same factual message can sound helpful, cold, angry, unsure, or respectful.

Too blunt:

> You did not send the file. Send it immediately.

Professional:

> I don't seem to have received the file yet. Could you please resend it when you have a moment?

Do not become so polite that the request disappears:

Vague:

> If possible, perhaps you might consider checking this sometime.

Clear and polite:

> Could you please check this by 3 p.m. today?

### 63.11 Positive wording

Negative-heavy:

> We cannot process your request because you did not attach the form.

More constructive:

> Please attach the completed form, and we'll be able to process your request.

### 63.12 Bullets and lists

Use bullets when the reader needs to scan several items.

> Please send the following by Friday:
>
> - signed approval form;
> - final cost sheet;
> - deployment date.

Keep list items grammatically parallel.

### 63.13 Attachments

Useful phrases:

- I've attached the report for your review.
- Please see the attached invoice.
- The requested file is attached.

Before sending, confirm that the attachment is actually present.

### 63.14 Closing lines

- Please let me know if you have any questions.
- Could you please confirm once this is completed?
- I look forward to your response.
- Thanks for your help.
- Please let me know what time works for you.

### 63.15 Sign-offs

Neutral/professional:

- Best regards,
- Kind regards,
- Regards,
- Best,

More formal:

- Sincerely,

### 63.16 Read the email aloud

Reading aloud helps reveal:

- sentences that are too long;
- a tone that sounds harsher than intended;
- repeated words;
- missing words;
- unnatural phrases.

### 63.17 Email may not be the best channel

Before sending a complex emotional or urgent email, ask whether a call, meeting, or chat would solve the issue faster. Follow up important verbal decisions in writing when a record is needed.

### 63.18 Mobile email caution

Quick mobile messages can become too short or contain errors. A device signature does not remove responsibility for clarity. Re-read important messages before sending.

### 63.19 Privacy and forwarding

Write emails with the understanding that they may be forwarded or stored. Avoid putting confidential, emotional, or insulting comments in email unless the channel and audience are appropriate.

### 63.20 Email examples

#### Requesting information

**Subject: UAT Access Required for Invoice Testing**

Hello Priya,

Could you please provide UAT access for the invoice testing activity? I need access to the Finance module and the test vendor data.

If possible, please enable it by tomorrow afternoon so I can complete testing before Friday.

Thanks for your help.

Best regards,  
Rahul

#### Sending an update

**Subject: Invoice Module - Testing Update**

Hi Team,

Testing is complete for the main invoice workflow. Two issues remain open:

- tax validation fails for one vendor scenario;
- the approval email is delayed by several minutes.

We're investigating both issues and will share the next update by 4 p.m. tomorrow.

Regards,  
Rahul

#### Reporting a delay

**Subject: Revised Delivery Date for Monthly Report**

Hello Neha,

The monthly report will be delayed by one day because we are still waiting for the final source data. I now expect to send the completed report by 2 p.m. tomorrow.

Sorry for the delay, and thank you for your patience.

Best regards,  
Rahul

#### Following up politely

**Subject: Follow-up: Approval Needed for UAT Deployment**

Hi Amit,

I'm following up on the UAT deployment approval shared yesterday. Could you please confirm whether we can proceed?

We are targeting deployment at 6 p.m. today, so a confirmation by 4 p.m. would help us keep the schedule.

Thanks,  
Rahul

### 63.21 Formal, neutral, and informal email style

The email workbook distinguishes three broad styles. In real life, they overlap, but the distinction is useful.

| Style | Typical situation | Language |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | complaint, legal/official matter, unfamiliar senior contact | careful, complete, more distant |
| Neutral/standard | most professional email | clear, direct, polite |
| Informal | friends or close colleagues | conversational, contractions, shorter expressions |

Compare:

- Formal: `I would be grateful if you could confirm the delivery date.`
- Neutral: `Could you please confirm the delivery date?`
- Informal: `Can you let me know when it'll arrive?`

Do not make a business email artificially formal with phrases such as `Please be informed that` or `Please find attached herewith` when a simpler sentence is clearer.

### 63.22 Missing words and abbreviations

Informal emails sometimes omit words that are obvious from context:

- `Hope you're well.` = `I hope you're well.`
- `Looking forward to seeing you.` = `I'm looking forward to seeing you.`
- `Sounds good.` = `That sounds good.`

This is normal in friendly email, but complete sentences are safer in formal or sensitive communication.

Useful conventional abbreviations:

- **i.e.** = that is / in other words
- **e.g.** = for example
- **NB** = note well / pay special attention
- **PS** = postscript, an added note after the main message

Text-message abbreviations such as `u`, `pls`, `thx`, or `asap` can be appropriate between people who already use them, but they can look careless in formal email. Match the reader and situation.

### 63.23 Opening and closing by purpose

| Purpose | Useful opening | Useful closing/action line |
|---|---|---|
| give news | `I wanted to update you on ...` | `I'll keep you posted.` |
| request information | `Could you please confirm ...?` | `I'd appreciate your reply by ...` |
| request action | `Could you please ...?` | `Please let me know once this is complete.` |
| offer help | `I'd be happy to help with ...` | `Let me know if you need anything else.` |
| internal update | `A quick update on ...` | `No action is needed at this stage.` |
| attachment | `I've attached ... for your review.` | `Please let me know if you cannot open it.` |
| meeting | `Could we meet to discuss ...?` | `Would Tuesday at 3 p.m. work for you?` |
| invitation | `I'd like to invite you to ...` | `Please confirm whether you can attend.` |
| directions | `The office is located ...` | `Call me if you have trouble finding it.` |
| check understanding | `Just to confirm, ...` | `Please correct me if I've misunderstood.` |

### 63.24 Arranging a meeting

A useful meeting email answers four questions: **why, who, when, and where/how**.

> **Subject:** Review meeting - invoice workflow - 21 August  
> Hi Priya,  
> Could we meet for 30 minutes to review the remaining invoice-workflow issues? I'm available on 21 August between 2:00 and 4:00 p.m. IST. We can meet on Teams.  
> Please let me know which time works for you.  
> Regards,  
> Rahul

If time zones matter, state them explicitly.

### 63.25 Invitations and directions

Useful patterns:

- We'd be pleased if you could join us for ...
- The session starts at 10:00 a.m.
- The building is opposite the metro station.
- Enter through Gate 2 and report to reception.
- Please let me know if you need a map or any assistance.

### 63.26 Negotiating a project or agreeing terms

Use language that separates **requirements** from **preferences**.

- Our main requirement is ...
- We would prefer ... if possible.
- Could you clarify whether the quoted price includes ...?
- We can agree to X provided that Y.
- That timeline would be difficult for us. Could we move the deadline to ...?
- To confirm our agreement: ...

After a negotiation, summarize the agreed points in writing so both sides have the same record.

### 63.27 Customer-supplier sequence

A typical commercial exchange may include:

1. inquiry;
2. quotation or availability response;
3. order;
4. order confirmation;
5. delivery/update;
6. invoice;
7. payment or payment follow-up;
8. complaint/return if a problem occurs.

Useful phrases:

- Please quote for ...
- Could you confirm availability and lead time?
- We would like to place an order for ...
- Please confirm receipt of our order.
- The goods are scheduled for dispatch on ...
- Please find the invoice attached.
- Our records show that invoice 123 remains outstanding.

### 63.28 Asking for payment

Be factual, polite, and specific. Do not accuse the reader unless you have verified the situation.

> **Subject:** Payment reminder - Invoice 123  
> Hi Arun,  
> Our records show that Invoice 123 for INR 25,000, due on 10 August, is still outstanding. Could you please check the status and let me know when payment is expected? If payment has already been made, please ignore this message and share the reference number if convenient.  
> Thank you.

### 63.29 Complaints

A useful complaint structure is:

1. state the problem;
2. give facts/evidence;
3. explain the effect;
4. state the remedy you want;
5. give a reasonable deadline if needed.

Example:

> We received order 482 on Monday, but 12 units were damaged. Photos are attached. Could you please arrange replacements by Friday or confirm an alternative solution?

Avoid emotional language such as `Your service is terrible` when a factual description will solve the problem more effectively.

### 63.30 Apologies

A professional apology should be clear about what happened and what happens next.

Useful structure:

> apology + responsibility/fact + correction + prevention/follow-up

- I'm sorry for the delay.
- We sent the wrong version of the file.
- I've attached the correct version now.
- We've also updated the checklist to prevent the same mistake.

Avoid a weak apology such as `Sorry if you were inconvenienced` when the inconvenience is known.

### 63.31 Describing business trends

Useful language:

- Sales **rose/increased** from 100 to 120.
- Sales **fell/decreased** by 10%.
- Costs **remained stable**.
- Demand **fluctuated** during the quarter.
- There was a **sharp increase** in April.
- There was a **gradual decline** over six months.

Remember:

- `increase by 20%` = amount of change;
- `increase to 120` = final level;
- `increase from 100 to 120` = start and finish.

### 63.32 Cause, effect, and contrast in reports/email

**Cause**

- because
- because of + noun
- due to + noun
- since/as, when the meaning is clear

**Effect**

- therefore
- so
- as a result
- consequently

**Contrast**

- but
- however
- although
- whereas/while
- on the other hand

Punctuation matters:

> The server was unavailable; therefore, the deployment was delayed.

Or more simply:

> The deployment was delayed because the server was unavailable.

### 63.33 Short business report structure

A short report can use:

1. **Purpose** - why the report exists;
2. **Findings** - what you found;
3. **Analysis** - what the findings mean;
4. **Recommendation/action** - what should happen next.

Useful signposts:

- The purpose of this report is to ...
- The main finding is ...
- This appears to be caused by ...
- Based on these findings, I recommend ...

### 63.34 Direct, indirect, and polite language

Being **direct** is not the same as being rude. Professional writing often benefits from a clear request.

Too indirect:

> I was wondering whether it might perhaps be possible for you to consider sending the report.

Clear and polite:

> Could you please send the report by 3 p.m.?

When refusing or giving bad news, a little indirectness can soften the message:

> Unfortunately, we won't be able to meet the original deadline. We can deliver by Thursday instead.

### 63.35 Friendly professional email

Professional email can sound human without becoming careless.

- Thanks for the quick update.
- I hope your week is going well.
- Great to hear that the issue is resolved.
- Have a good weekend.

Use friendliness naturally. Do not add personal comments that are inappropriate for the relationship or situation.

### 63.36 Advice and suggestions by email

Use different strengths:

- You **could** try ... *(option)*
- You **might want to** ... *(gentle suggestion)*
- I **recommend** ... *(clear recommendation)*
- You **should** ... *(stronger advice)*
- We **need to** ... *(necessity)*

Explain the reason when the suggestion affects the reader's work.

### 63.37 Job-application email

Keep the email short. The resume/CV carries the detail.

> **Subject:** Application - Software Engineer - Rahul Khan  
> Dear Hiring Manager,  
> I'm applying for the Software Engineer position advertised on your careers page. I have experience in web application development, APIs, and relational databases, and the role closely matches my recent work. My resume is attached for your review.  
> I'd welcome the opportunity to discuss how my experience could contribute to the team.  
> Kind regards,  
> Rahul Khan

Do not write a generic message that gives the employer no reason to open the resume.

### 63.38 Inbox and message-management habits

The source also treats email as a work process, not only a writing exercise. Useful habits include:

- act on short messages when practical;
- delete or archive messages that no longer need attention;
- forward only when the recipient genuinely needs the information;
- save/file messages that form an important record;
- avoid constant notification checking when it damages concentration;
- verify recipients before sending sensitive information.

The exact system matters less than having a consistent way to distinguish **action**, **reference**, and **noise**.

## 64. Paragraph Writing

A clear paragraph usually has one central idea.

### Simple paragraph structure

1. Topic sentence
2. Explanation
3. Example/evidence
4. Closing/link sentence

Example:

> Daily speaking practice is one of the fastest ways to become more comfortable with English. Grammar study gives you rules, but speaking practice teaches you to retrieve those rules in real time. For example, a learner can describe the same work task for two minutes every day and gradually use more natural phrases. Consistent short practice is usually more useful than speaking for one hour only once a month.

### Avoid giant paragraphs

If a paragraph contains several separate topics, divide it. Shorter paragraphs improve readability, especially in email and online writing.

## 65. Essay and Speech Planning

The spoken-English material includes frameworks for generating ideas. A framework helps when you understand the language but do not know **what to say**.

### 65.1 PREP method

**P - Point**  
**R - Reason**  
**E - Example**  
**P - Point again**

Example topic: *Working from home can be useful.*

> **Point:** Working from home can improve productivity for some employees.  
> **Reason:** It removes commuting time and can reduce office distractions.  
> **Example:** An employee who normally spends two hours travelling may use part of that time for focused work or rest.  
> **Point:** Therefore, when the job and home environment are suitable, remote work can be an effective option.

### 65.2 ERP variation

**Example -> Reason -> Point** can help if an example comes to mind first.

### 65.3 Perspective checklist: I GET HELPS

The spoken-English source uses the memory aid **I GET HELPS** for generating content:

- **I - International**
- **G - Geographical**
- **E - Environmental**
- **T - Technical/technological**
- **H - Historical**
- **E - Economic**
- **L - Legal**
- **P - Political**
- **S - Social**

For each relevant angle, consider both **positive** and **negative** effects. You do not need to use every letter. The framework is a brainstorming tool, not a requirement for every essay. Choose only perspectives that genuinely fit the topic and that you can explain with evidence or examples.

### 65.4 Basic speech structure

**Opening** - hook + topic  
**Body** - 2-3 main points + examples  
**Closing** - summary + final message

Example opening:

> Most of us check our phones dozens of times a day, but how often do we ask whether those checks are helping us? Today I'd like to discuss how small changes in phone use can improve concentration.

### 65.5 Signposting language

- First, ...
- The main reason is ...
- For example, ...
- Another important point is ...
- However, ...
- On the other hand, ...
- As a result, ...
- To sum up, ...

## 66. Group Discussion English

A good group discussion is not a competition to speak the most. It tests **content, listening, clarity, teamwork, and the ability to move the discussion forward**.

### 66.1 Before speaking

Listen to the exact topic. Define key terms and scope.

### 66.2 Useful entry phrases

- I'd like to add one point.
- Building on what Priya said, ...
- I agree with that, and I'd also add ...
- I see it slightly differently.
- May I add an example here?
- We haven't yet considered ...

### 66.3 If you speak first

You do not have to speak first. If you do, define the topic and give the group a useful direction rather than making a dramatic speech.

> I think we can look at this topic from three angles: cost, employee productivity, and long-term impact. I'll start with productivity.

### 66.4 If the group becomes noisy

Do not try to shout over everyone. Wait for a small pause and say:

- Can we take one point at a time?
- I think several people are trying to speak. Shall we let Priya finish first?
- We seem to be moving away from the main topic. Can we return to ...?

### 66.5 Disagreeing professionally

- I understand that view, but I disagree for two reasons.
- That's a valid concern; however, the data may lead to a different conclusion.
- I agree with the first point, but not the second.

### 66.6 Summarizing

A **summary** reports what the group discussed. A **conclusion** states the final position. They are not always the same.

Useful summary language:

> We discussed three main points: cost, productivity, and employee well-being. There was broad agreement that flexibility is useful, although we differed on how many remote days should be allowed.

### 66.6A Entry strategy and the "fish market" situation

The source calls an uncontrolled, noisy group discussion a **fish market**. The useful lesson is not the label; it is how to respond.

- Do not create the noise by interrupting repeatedly.
- Do not try to win by shouting.
- Wait for a small pause or for repeated points.
- Enter by connecting to the previous speaker: `I agree with that point, and I'd add ...`
- If needed, calm the discussion: `Can we take one person at a time?`
- Bring the group back to the topic: `We seem to be moving away from the main question.`

The source also notes that speaking first does **not** automatically earn extra credit. A good second or later speaker can add more value by broadening, clarifying, or organizing the discussion.

**Summary vs conclusion:** A summary should not introduce new arguments. A conclusion can close the discussion by drawing a final position from what has already been discussed.

### 66.7 Common mistakes

- interrupting constantly;
- repeating someone else's point without adding value;
- speaking for too long;
- attacking a person instead of an idea;
- giving unsupported claims as facts;
- ignoring quieter participants;
- using memorized vocabulary that does not fit the topic.

# Part X - Interview and Professional English

## 67. Interview Communication

The interview ebook includes common interview questions, self-introduction, mock interviews, extempore speaking, and resume guidance. The most important principle is this:

> **Do not memorize impressive sentences that are not true. Prepare clear, specific examples from your actual experience.**

### 67.1 Tell me about yourself

A useful professional structure:

1. current role/area;
2. relevant experience or strengths;
3. important work or achievement;
4. what you are looking for next.

Example:

> I'm a software engineer with experience building and supporting web applications. My work mainly involves backend APIs, databases, and frontend integration. In my current role, I've worked on workflow automation and business applications used by internal teams. I enjoy solving operational problems through software, and I'm now looking for a role where I can take on larger technical responsibilities and continue developing my engineering skills.

Avoid beginning with unrelated personal details unless the interviewer asks.

### 67.2 What are your strengths?

Weak answer:

> I am hardworking, honest, punctual, and loyal.

These adjectives are easy to claim and hard to prove.

Stronger pattern:

> Strength + evidence + result

Example:

> One of my strengths is troubleshooting. When an application issue crosses frontend, API, and database layers, I can usually narrow down the cause systematically. For example, in a recent production issue, I traced an incorrect status from the UI to an API response and then to a database condition, which helped the team fix it quickly.

### 67.3 What is your weakness?

Choose a real but manageable development area. Explain what you are doing about it.

> Earlier, I sometimes spent too long polishing technical details before sharing progress. I've been improving this by giving earlier status updates and separating “must fix now” items from improvements that can wait.

Avoid fake weaknesses such as `I work too hard` unless they are genuinely meaningful and supported.

### 67.4 Why should we hire you?

Connect your experience to the employer's needs.

> My experience matches the role in three areas: full-stack development, production troubleshooting, and working directly with business users. I can contribute technically, but I'm also comfortable understanding a workflow, identifying the real problem, and explaining solutions to non-technical stakeholders.

### 67.5 Why do you want to join this company?

Use specific reasons:

- role scope;
- product/domain;
- technical challenges;
- learning opportunities;
- company direction/culture, if you have evidence.

Avoid:

> Because your company is very famous and I want growth.

Better:

> I'm interested in this role because it combines backend engineering with workflow automation, which is close to the work I've enjoyed most. The scale of the systems described in the job description would also give me an opportunity to deepen my experience with distributed services.

### 67.6 Where do you see yourself in five years?

You do not need to predict an exact job title.

> In five years, I'd like to be a strong senior engineer who can own important systems end to end, mentor others, and contribute to technical decisions. I'm flexible about the exact title; the main goal is to grow in responsibility and technical depth.

### 67.7 Why are you leaving your current job?

Keep the answer factual and forward-looking.

> I've learned a lot in my current role, but I'm looking for broader technical ownership and stronger opportunities to work on scalable engineering problems.

Avoid attacking your employer or colleagues.

### 67.8 Salary questions

Useful language:

- I'm open to discussing a package that is appropriate for the responsibilities of the role and my experience.
- Based on the role and the market, I'm targeting a range of ...
- Could you share the budgeted range for the position?

Be ready to clarify whether you mean fixed pay, total compensation, bonus, or other components.

### 67.9 When you do not know an answer

Do not bluff.

> I haven't worked with that directly, so I don't want to guess. My current understanding is ..., and I would verify ... before implementing it.

Or:

> I'm not sure of the exact answer. Would it be okay if I explain how I would approach finding it?

### 67.10 Clarifying a question

- Could you clarify what you mean by ...?
- Are you asking about my current project or my overall experience?
- Would you like a technical explanation or a high-level overview?

This is better than answering the wrong question.

### 67.11 Can you work under pressure?

A weak answer is only `Yes, I can.` A stronger answer explains **how** you work under pressure.

> Yes. When priorities become urgent, I first confirm what is truly time-critical, break the work into smaller steps, communicate risks early, and focus on one priority at a time. For example, during a production issue, I pause lower-priority work, keep stakeholders updated, and document the fix after service is restored.

Do not claim that pressure never affects you. Employers are usually testing your judgment, prioritization, and communication.

### 67.12 Do you have any questions for us?

Prepare two or three genuine questions. Good areas include:

- What would success look like in the first three to six months?
- What are the most important technical challenges for this team?
- How is the team structured?
- How are engineering decisions reviewed?
- What are the next steps in the interview process?

Questions only about leave, salary, or office timings can be necessary, but they should not be your **only** questions when you have an opportunity to understand the role.

### 67.13 General self-introduction language bank

The interview source also teaches personal-information sentences. These are useful outside interviews too.

- My name is ...
- I'm from ...
- I live in ...
- I work as a ... / I work in ...
- I studied ... at ...
- I graduated in ...
- I have ... years of experience in ...
- My main interests are ...
- In my free time, I enjoy ...
- I would describe myself as calm and curious.

In a professional interview, include only personal details that help answer the question. You normally do **not** need to volunteer age, marital status, religion, family size, phone number, or full home address.

## 68. The STAR Method for Behavioral Questions

Use **STAR** when asked for a specific example.

- **S - Situation:** What was happening?
- **T - Task:** What was your responsibility?
- **A - Action:** What did you personally do?
- **R - Result:** What happened because of your action?

Question:

> Tell me about a time you solved a difficult problem.

Answer skeleton:

> **Situation:** A production workflow was failing only for one category of invoices.  
> **Task:** I was responsible for identifying whether the issue was in the UI, API, or database.  
> **Action:** I reproduced the issue, compared a successful and failed request, traced the status values through the API, and found that one database condition did not handle the new document type. I corrected the condition and added a regression test.  
> **Result:** The affected workflow started processing correctly, and the added test reduced the chance of the same issue returning.

### STAR mistakes

- spending 80% of the answer on background;
- saying only `we` when the interviewer needs to know your contribution;
- giving no result;
- choosing a story unrelated to the skill being tested;
- inventing numbers you cannot defend.

## 69. Extempore Speaking

The interview source uses an exercise where a learner speaks continuously on random words. This is useful for building mental flexibility, but quality is more important than filling time with meaningless words.

### A simple 60-second structure

For a random topic such as **mobile phones**:

1. define it;
2. explain why it matters;
3. give one benefit;
4. give one drawback;
5. give an example;
6. conclude.

Example:

> Mobile phones are now an essential communication tool for most people. They allow us to call, message, navigate, make payments, and access information almost anywhere. One major advantage is convenience, especially during travel or emergencies. However, constant notifications can also reduce concentration. For example, many people check their phones while working even when the message is not urgent. In my view, mobile phones are extremely useful when we control how we use them.

### Linking phrases for extempore speaking

- To begin with, ...
- One reason is ...
- For instance, ...
- At the same time, ...
- Another point is ...
- Overall, ...

If you need a second to think:

> That's an interesting topic. I would look at it from two sides.

This sounds better than long silence or repeating `actually` many times.

## 70. Resume Language Basics

A resume should communicate evidence quickly. Use clear action verbs and specific results where possible.

### Weak bullet

> Responsible for handling application issues.

### Stronger bullet

> Diagnosed and resolved application issues across frontend, API, and database layers.

### Better with measurable result, when truthful

> Automated a manual reconciliation process, reducing average processing time from 45 minutes to 10 minutes.

Never invent metrics. If exact numbers are unavailable, describe scope honestly:

> Automated a recurring reconciliation process used by the Finance team.

### Useful resume action verbs

- built
- developed
- designed
- implemented
- automated
- optimized
- reduced
- improved
- migrated
- integrated
- resolved
- analyzed
- led
- coordinated
- delivered
- documented
- tested
- monitored
- supported

### Avoid weak filler

Instead of:

> Worked on development of APIs.

Use:

> Developed REST APIs for invoice processing and approval workflows.

### Tense in a resume

- current responsibilities: present tense can be used (`Develop`, `Maintain`);
- completed achievements/past jobs: past tense (`Developed`, `Reduced`, `Led`).

Keep style consistent.

### 70.1 Seven-part resume structure

The resume source presents a practical seven-step sequence. In updated form:

1. **Name and contact information** - name, phone, professional email, city/region, and relevant profile link such as LinkedIn.
2. **Headline** - a short description of your professional identity, tailored to the role when useful.
3. **Professional summary** - a concise overview of relevant experience, strengths, and achievements.
4. **Work experience** - usually reverse chronological; include employer, role, dates, relevant responsibilities, and results.
5. **Relevant skills and keywords** - use genuine skills that match the job description; do not keyword-stuff.
6. **Education, certifications, awards, or other relevant information** - include what supports the application.
7. **Grammar and formatting review** - check tense, punctuation, spacing, dates, consistency, and readability.

A resume headline should be specific enough to help the reader understand your profile quickly. A summary should not simply repeat the headline. Work-experience bullets should emphasize **action + scope + result** where truthful.

### 70.2 Resume consistency checklist

Before sending:

- Are dates formatted consistently?
- Are current-role bullets and past-role bullets using consistent tense?
- Are role titles and company names easy to scan?
- Are the most relevant achievements near the top?
- Have you removed vague claims such as `hardworking` unless evidence supports them?
- Have you checked every metric and technical claim for accuracy?
- Is the filename professional, for example `Rahul_Khan_Resume.pdf`?

# Part XI - Common Mistakes and Teacher's Corrections

## 71. Corrections Found While Reviewing the Learning Material

This section is intentionally explicit. Some examples in the source material teach the right general topic but use grammar or wording that should **not** be memorized unchanged.

### Articles and simple present

Source-style:

> The Sun Rise In The East.

Correct:

> The sun rises in the east.

Why:

- common noun *sun* is not normally capitalized mid-sentence;
- third-person singular subject *the sun* needs *rises*.

### Article before consonant sound

Source-style:

> an hair

Correct:

> a hair

Because the *h* in *hair* is pronounced.

### Uncountable *help*

Source-style:

> Manish need a help.

Correct:

> Manish needs help.

Or:

> Manish needs some help.

### One-sided

Source-style:

> a one side decision

Better:

> a one-sided decision

`One` begins with a /w/ sound, so *a* is correct, and *one-sided* is the adjective.

### Past passive

Source-style:

> Ram watch a movie. -> A movie was watched by Ram.

Correct active:

> Ram watched a movie.

The passive is then:

> A movie was watched by Ram.

### Present perfect with finished time

Source-style:

> We have visited Europe in last year.

Correct:

> We visited Europe last year.

Use the simple past with a finished time such as *last year*.

### Stative verb

Source-style:

> I am loving you.

Normally:

> I love you.

The continuous form may occur in advertising, playful language, or special contexts, but it is not the standard beginner rule for the state meaning of *love*.

### Forced passive

Source-style:

> Ram is talking with Mohan. -> Mohan is being talked with Ram.

Natural English keeps the active sentence:

> Ram is talking with Mohan.

Not every sentence should be forced into a passive form.

### Do-support

Source-style:

> He does not teaches me English.

Correct:

> He does not teach me English.

After *does*, use V1.

### Double-object passive

Source-style:

> You were not written a letter by them.

This construction is at best highly unnatural. Prefer:

> They did not write you a letter.

Or, if a passive is needed:

> A letter was not written to you.

### Past participle of show

Source-style:

> Let the identity be showed.

Standard:

> Let the identity be shown.

### Misunderstanding someone

Source-style:

> You are taking me wrong.

Natural:

> You are misunderstanding me.

Or:

> You have misunderstood me.

### Interview English

Source-style:

> What do you do for living?

Correct:

> What do you do for a living?

### Customer-service wording

Source-style:

> If the customer abuses, we have to politely deal.

Better:

> If a customer becomes abusive, we should remain calm, set appropriate boundaries, and follow the company's escalation policy.

This is both more natural English and more professional guidance.

### Mobile description

Source-style:

> These mobiles are having facilities like music, video, games and lot many other things.

Natural:

> These phones have features such as music, video, games, and many other tools.

### Weight

Source-style:

> I put up 10 Kgs.

Natural:

> I put on 10 kg.

Or:

> I gained 10 kg.

### Care

Source-style phrasal expression:

> see after

Modern natural choice:

> look after

### Article rule correction

A source explanation says articles are used only before singular nouns. That is true only for **a/an** with singular countable nouns. **The** can be used with singular nouns, plural nouns, and uncountable nouns:

- the book
- the books on the shelf
- the information you requested

## 72. Frequent Grammar Mistakes Learners Should Watch

### Missing third-person -s

- Wrong: She work here.
- Right: She works here.

### Using V2 after did

- Wrong: Did he came?
- Right: Did he come?

### Using V3 without an auxiliary where one is required

- Wrong: I finished? *(fine only as a statement; not a perfect form)*
- Wrong perfect: I finished the work already since morning.
- Better: I have already finished the work.

### Confusing *since* and *for*

- Wrong: for Monday
- Right: since Monday

- Wrong: since three hours
- Right: for three hours

### Countable/uncountable errors

- Wrong: many furniture
- Right: much furniture / a lot of furniture

- Wrong: an information
- Right: some information / a piece of information

### Double comparative

- Wrong: more easier
- Right: easier

### Preposition after *discuss*

- Wrong: discuss about
- Right: discuss

### Missing article with singular countable noun

- Wrong: I bought laptop.
- Right: I bought a laptop.

### Wrong adjective/adverb

- Wrong: He speaks very slow.
- Right: He speaks very slowly.

### Unnecessary continuous

- Wrong: I am understanding.
- Right: I understand.

### Wrong question word order

- Wrong: Where you are going?
- Right: Where are you going?

### Indirect-question inversion

- Wrong: Can you tell me where is he?
- Right: Can you tell me where he is?

# Part XIV - Quick Reference

## 108. The 12 Tense Formulas

`S` = subject, `V1` = base form, `V2` = past form, `V3` = past participle.

| Tense | Affirmative | Negative | Yes/no question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present simple | S + V1/V1+s | S + do/does not + V1 | Do/Does + S + V1? |
| Present continuous | S + am/is/are + V-ing | S + am/is/are not + V-ing | Am/Is/Are + S + V-ing? |
| Present perfect | S + has/have + V3 | S + has/have not + V3 | Has/Have + S + V3? |
| Present perfect continuous | S + has/have been + V-ing | S + has/have not been + V-ing | Has/Have + S + been + V-ing? |
| Past simple | S + V2 | S + did not + V1 | Did + S + V1? |
| Past continuous | S + was/were + V-ing | S + was/were not + V-ing | Was/Were + S + V-ing? |
| Past perfect | S + had + V3 | S + had not + V3 | Had + S + V3? |
| Past perfect continuous | S + had been + V-ing | S + had not been + V-ing | Had + S + been + V-ing? |
| Future simple | S + will + V1 | S + will not + V1 | Will + S + V1? |
| Future continuous | S + will be + V-ing | S + will not be + V-ing | Will + S + be + V-ing? |
| Future perfect | S + will have + V3 | S + will not have + V3 | Will + S + have + V3? |
| Future perfect continuous | S + will have been + V-ing | S + will not have been + V-ing | Will + S + have been + V-ing? |

For a **WH-question**, place the WH-word before the yes/no-question structure:

- Where **does she work**?
- Why **were they waiting**?
- How long **have you been studying**?

A subject question is different: `Who called you?` does not use *did* because **who** is the subject.

## 114. Speaking Quick Checklist

When you get stuck:

1. slow down;
2. use a shorter sentence;
3. explain with different words;
4. give an example;
5. use a filler only if useful: `Let me think`;
6. ask for clarification if needed;
7. continue - do not abandon the conversation because of one mistake.

### What "complete" means in this handbook

For **teaching chapters**, important rules, exceptions, patterns, distinctions, practical lessons, and exercise types are included or cross-referenced. For **dictionary/list books** containing hundreds or thousands of independent entries, completeness means that the learning method and important/high-value entries are represented; it does **not** mean reproducing every row. Copying every vocabulary row would add enormous duplication without adding a new concept.

### Final verification principles used

- Source material was not assumed to be correct merely because it appeared in a PDF.
- A source example that contains an error is either corrected or clearly labeled as a source-style error.
- Repeated grammar explanations are merged into one stronger explanation.
- Hindi-dependent recognition shortcuts are not treated as universal grammar rules.
- Modern, broadly understandable English is preferred when the source wording is awkward or highly regional.
- Rare exam vocabulary is identified as such rather than presented as essential everyday speech.
- Newly written exercises/examples are teaching additions; they are not presented as quotations from the PDFs.

## 135. How Source Errors Were Handled

The books contain useful learning material but also occasional grammar errors, awkward translations, outdated expressions, or examples that demonstrate a rule poorly. These were **not preserved blindly**. Where an example was useful, it was corrected and the reason was explained. Where a transformation was unnatural - especially some passive-voice examples - the handbook explains why the active sentence should remain active.

This matters because repetition creates habits. A learner should not repeatedly practise an incorrect sentence simply because it appeared in a study book.

# Part XVIII - Final Mastery Checklist

## 136. Beginner Level

You are ready to move beyond beginner level when you can usually:

- make simple subject-verb-object sentences;
- use *be, have,* and *do* correctly in common patterns;
- distinguish present, past, and future basic forms;
- use articles with common nouns reasonably well;
- ask basic yes/no and WH questions;
- give simple commands and polite requests;
- introduce yourself and handle a short everyday conversation;
- understand common countable/uncountable differences.

## 137. Intermediate Level

You are progressing through intermediate level when you can:

- choose between simple, continuous, and perfect forms based on meaning;
- use modals for advice, obligation, permission, and possibility;
- report what someone said;
- use passive voice when it has a purpose;
- connect ideas with subordinate clauses;
- use conditionals;
- write a clear professional email;
- explain an opinion with reasons and examples;
- use common phrasal verbs and collocations naturally;
- correct many of your own recurring errors.

## 138. Advanced Practical Level

Advanced practical English is not defined by using rare words. It means you can:

- adapt tone to formal, neutral, and informal situations;
- express precise differences in time, certainty, cause, and attitude;
- organize long explanations clearly;
- handle disagreement without losing clarity or professionalism;
- paraphrase when you do not know a word;
- understand why two grammatical sentences may differ in naturalness;
- write concisely without sounding abrupt;
- use vocabulary according to context and collocation;
- notice regional, formal, informal, and outdated expressions;
- communicate confidently even when a sentence is not perfect.

# Final Advice

Do not try to memorize this handbook from top to bottom in one pass. English becomes usable through **repeated retrieval**: read a rule, say examples aloud, create your own examples, use the pattern in a real situation, make a mistake, correct it, and use it again.

A useful rule to remember is:

> **Clear + correct + natural beats complicated.**

If you can express a difficult idea in simple, accurate English, your English is already doing its job.
